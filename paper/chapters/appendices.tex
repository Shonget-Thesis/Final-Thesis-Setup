\section[APPENDIX A.\ SAMPLE CALCULATIONS]{Sample Calculations}
\label{app:calc}

This appendix follows one input through the planned method, from the cost of each system to the calibration of the threshold, using the formulas of Chapter~\ref{sec:method}. The numbers for the prompt, the mel-spectrogram values, the scores and the failure counts are \emph{illustrative}: they are chosen to be easy to follow and are not results of the study. The grid points, the costs and the sample-size limit follow directly from the definitions.

\subsection{Step 1: Cost of the Released System and of Each Candidate Path}
\label{app:calc-cost}

The released DMOSpeech~2 sampler (Figure~\ref{fig:rrl-tg}) works as follows. Its teacher takes eight guided Euler steps on the 16-interval grid, evaluates the endpoint once more, and hands over to the student, which finishes in three steps. With \Cref{eq:cost}, $e=8+1=9$, $u=0$ and $n_s=3$:
\[
C_{\text{ref}} = 2\cdot 9 + 0 + 3 = 21 \text{ forward passes.}
\]

The proposed router (Figure~\ref{fig:method}) runs its teacher on the 8-interval grid and stops after $k$ steps at the commit point $t_k=1-\cos(\pi k/16)$ (\Cref{eq:paths}). It then needs one endpoint evaluation, the student draft ($u=1$), and $4-k/2$ student steps, so $C(k)=2(k+1)+1+(4-k/2)$. Table~\ref{tab:app-cost} applies these formulas to the three candidate paths, with the saving of \Cref{eq:saving}.

\begin{table}[!htbp]
\centering\small
\caption{Commit point, cost and saving of each candidate path}
\label{tab:app-cost}
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}c c c c c c@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Path $k$} & \textbf{Commit point $t_k$} & \textbf{Teacher $2(k+1)$} & \textbf{Draft} & \textbf{Student $4-k/2$} & \textbf{$C(k)$ (saving)} \\

2 & 0.0761 & 6 & 1 & 3 & 10 (52\%) \\

For example, the four-step path saves $\Delta C = 1-13/21 = 0.381$, that is, 38\% of the released system's forward passes. The commit points are also points of the student's schedule, $\tau_{k/2}$, which is why the student can restart exactly where the teacher stops.

\subsection{Step 2: Prompt SNR Estimate}
\label{app:calc-snr}

Suppose that, after the prompt is cut into 25~ms pieces, the loudest half of the pieces has a mean energy of $\bar E_{\text{top}}=2.0\times10^{-2}$ and the quietest 10\% has $\bar E_{\text{bottom}}=1.0\times10^{-4}$. By \Cref{eq:snr},
\[
\hat S = 10\log_{10}\frac{2.0\times10^{-2}}{1.0\times10^{-4}} = 10\log_{10}200 \approx 23.0\text{ dB}.
\]
This is above the gate $\theta=20$~dB, so the prompt does not look noisy, and the decision is left to the residual.

\subsection{Step 3: Quantile Projection}
\label{app:calc-quantile}

The projection works band by band. Take one frequency band over four generated frames. The teacher's estimate and the student draft in that band are
\[
\hat x_1 = (2.0,\ 5.0,\ 3.0,\ 1.0), \qquad s_0 = (0.5,\ 1.5,\ 1.0,\ 2.5).
\]
In the teacher's estimate, frame~4 is the quietest, then frames~1 and~3, and frame~2 is the loudest. The draft's values, sorted, are $0.5<1.0<1.5<2.5$. The projection gives the smallest draft value to the teacher's quietest frame, the next to the next, and so on (\Cref{eq:quantile}):
\[
Q(\hat x_1,s_0) = (1.0,\ 2.5,\ 1.5,\ 0.5).
\]
The order in time is the teacher's (frame~2 is still the loudest, frame~4 the quietest), but the values are the draft's. This is how the projection keeps the teacher's timing while removing loudness values that the teacher may have copied from a noisy prompt.

\subsection{Step 4: Invariant Residual}
\label{app:calc-residual}

The residual compares the teacher's estimate with its projection (\Cref{eq:residual}). For the band above,
\[
\hat x_1-Q = (1.0,\ 2.5,\ 1.5,\ 0.5),
\]
\[
\lVert\hat x_1-Q\rVert=\sqrt{1.0^2+2.5^2+1.5^2+0.5^2}=\sqrt{9.75}\approx3.12,
\]
\[
\lVert\hat x_1\rVert=\sqrt{2.0^2+5.0^2+3.0^2+1.0^2}=\sqrt{39}\approx6.24,\qquad r\approx\frac{3.12}{6.24}=0.50.
\]
In the method the sums run over all generated frames and all bands, but the calculation is the same. A residual of 0.50 means that the teacher's values differ from the draft's by half of their own size. If the teacher's values had been the draft's values in a different order, the residual would be zero (Proposition~\ref{prop:null}).

\subsection{Step 5: Routing Decision and Student Finish}
\label{app:calc-route}

Suppose the calibration has set the threshold of the four-step path to $\hat\lambda_4=0.25$ (Step~7 below). The routing rule (\Cref{eq:policy}) checks two conditions:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\hat S &= 23.0\ \ge\ \theta = 20 && \text{(gate passed)},\\
r &= 0.50\ >\ \hat\lambda_4 = 0.25 && \text{(residual too large)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Because the residual is above the threshold, the router hands the student the projection $Q(\hat x_1,s_0)$. The student then finishes with $4-k/2=2$ steps from $\tau_2=t_4$. Had the residual been, say, 0.20, the router would have handed over the teacher's own estimate; had the SNR been below 20~dB, it would have handed over the projection whatever the residual. In every case the input costs $C(4)=13$ forward passes.

\subsection{Step 7: Calibrating the Threshold of One Path}
\label{app:calc-ltt}

Calibration repeats Step~6 for every calibration input and every candidate threshold, using the recorded outputs, and counts the excess failures. Suppose that, on $n=200$ calibration inputs, the four-step path gives the counts of Table~\ref{tab:app-ltt}. With $\alpha=0.05$, each p-value is $p=\P(\mathrm{Bin}(200,0.05)\le m)$ for $m$ excess failures (\Cref{eq:pvalue}), and a threshold is accepted when $p\le\delta/3=0.10/3\approx0.033$.

\centering\small
\caption{Fixed-sequence test for the four-step path with illustrative counts}
\label{tab:app-ltt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}c c c c@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Threshold $\lambda$} & \textbf{Excess failures $m$} & \textbf{p-value} & \textbf{Decision} \\
\midrule
0.00 & 0 & $<0.0001$ & accept \\
0.05 & 0 & $<0.0001$ & accept \\
0.10 & 1 & 0.0004 & accept \\
0.15 & 2 & 0.0023 & accept \\
0.20 & 3 & 0.0090 & accept \\
0.25 & 4 & 0.0264 & accept \\
0.30 & 5 & 0.0623 & stop \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The thresholds are tested from the safest, which projects the most inputs, to the least safe, and the test stops at the first threshold that is not accepted. Here that is $\lambda=0.30$, so the calibrated threshold is the last accepted one, $\hat\lambda_4=0.25$. The same test is run separately for the two- and six-step paths. With confidence at least 90\%, all three calibrated paths then add failures on at most 5\% of new inputs (Proposition~\ref{prop:ltt}).

\subsection{Step 8: Minimum Number of Calibration Speakers}
\label{app:calc-size}

Even with no excess failures at all, a threshold can be accepted only if $(1-\alpha)^n\le\delta/3$ (\Cref{eq:zerofail}). With $\alpha=0.05$ and $\delta=0.10$,
\[
n \ \ge\ \frac{\ln(0.10/3)}{\ln(0.95)} \approx 66.3,
\]
so at least 67 calibration speakers are needed; for a single path ($\delta$ instead of $\delta/3$) the limit would be 45. With 200 calibration speakers, Table~\ref{tab:app-ltt} shows that a threshold can still be accepted with up to four excess failures.

\section[APPENDIX B.\ PRELIMINARY EXPERIMENTS]{Preliminary Experiments}
\label{app:prelim}

This appendix reports the team's earlier experiments that led to the payload router. All of them were run on Google Colab Tesla T4 GPUs with the evaluation models of Section~\ref{sec:measures}. They are development evidence: they guided the design but are not evidence for the final method, which will be calibrated and tested on new speakers (Chapter~\ref{sec:method}).

\subsection{A Fixed Early Handover Fails on Noisy Prompts}
\label{app:prelim-fixed}

The concept-defense method aimed to move the handover earlier. The first experiment used 500 speakers, each under a clean and a 15~dB noisy prompt, and gave every input the same earlier switching time. Table~\ref{tab:fixed-fail} counts the inputs on which the output became clearly worse than at the released switching time. Earlier handovers saved computation but failed on about half or more of the noisy prompts, while clean prompts were almost never affected. Choosing the cheapest safe switching time for each input in hindsight would have needed 14.85 forward passes on average instead of 21, which showed that a saving was possible if the noisy prompts could be handled.

\subsection{Adapting the Handover Time Could Not Be Certified}
\label{app:prelim-timing}

The concept-defense method chose the handover time for each input with two signals: the prompt SNR estimate, with a 20~dB gate, and an endpoint-stabilization score that measured how much the teacher's estimate still changed between steps,
\[
d_i = \frac{\|\hat x_1(t_i) - \hat x_1(t_{i-1})\|_M}{\|\hat x_1(t_i)\|_M + \epsilon},
\]
handing over at the first step where $d_i$ fell below a threshold. Three results ended this approach:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{No certified saving.} Calibrated with Learn-Then-Test over six prompt conditions, the rule accepted no threshold, so it saved nothing: every input kept the full 21 forward passes.
\item \textbf{Noise made the teacher look settled.} Noise weakened the difference between the teacher's guided and unguided directions, so the stabilization score fell: its median dropped from 0.61 on clean prompts to 0.17 on 15~dB noisy prompts. The rule therefore handed over noisy prompts first, the opposite of what was needed.
\item \textbf{The SNR gate missed moderate damage.} Prompts with 18 to 22~dB noise were often estimated above 20~dB and passed the gate, causing 86 added failures in 360 inputs (23.9\%). Telephone-band prompts, whose pauses are silenced, were read as about 44~dB.
\end{enumerate}
These results showed that the problem was not when the teacher hands over but what it hands over: on damaged prompts, the teacher's estimate carries the prompt's noise into the student's output.

\subsection{Development Run of the Payload Router}
\label{app:snr-r}

The payload router was then tested on 30 development speakers under the eight prompt conditions of Table~\ref{tab:conditions}, with the four-step path, the 20~dB gate and a residual threshold of 0.34 fixed beforehand from a small screen. Table~\ref{tab:snr-r-overall} compares it with the two signals of the earlier method, with each payload used alone, with the released system and with the student restarted from its own draft.

\begin{tabular}{@{}l r r r r r@{}}

\bottomrule

Three findings shaped Chapter~\ref{sec:method}:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{The residual detects damage that the SNR gate misses.} As a detector of inputs that needed the projection, the residual reached an area under the ROC curve of 0.864, against 0.697 for the stabilization score and 0.538 for the SNR estimate. The router's UTMOS was 0.57 higher than that of the SNR gate alone, with the largest gains on the moderate 25~dB noises.
\item \textbf{Routing keeps the teacher's variety on clean prompts.} On clean prompts, the router kept 96\% of the teacher's added pitch variety (time-aligned), whereas always projecting kept 87\%. This is why the router chooses between the two payloads instead of always correcting.
\item \textbf{The student alone is a strong competitor.} The student restarted from its own draft had the best quality at 3 passes but the least pitch variety. Chapter~\ref{sec:method} therefore includes it as a compared system and fixes in advance a test of whether the router's variety justifies the teacher's passes.
\end{enumerate}
The run used few speakers and a threshold chosen without a statistical test, so these figures are not evidence for the final method. Severe reverberation and telephone-band prompts remained difficult for every system.
