\subsection{Background of the Study}
\label{sec:background}

Giving computers a voice is the goal of text-to-speech (TTS) technology, which turns written text into spoken output while aiming to keep the speech clear, true to the speaker's voice, and natural. One approach used in generative TTS systems is flow matching, which describes a smooth change from random noise into a representation of speech. Flow-matching models learn how to move along a path that connects noise to speech, and this path is often fairly simple or close to a straight line. The learned directions are then followed through a series of calculations to produce speech \parencite{lipman2023flow,liu2023flow}. F5-TTS, developed by \textcite{chen2024f5}, uses flow matching through a Diffusion Transformer and introduces Sway Sampling, which places more of the solver's steps early in generation, where the overall structure of the speech is decided. These developments form a basis for studying ways to generate speech more efficiently, especially because a model's full computing cost may not be needed for every input.

Despite these advances, generating speech can still require many repeated calculations by the neural network. This increases computing cost and may slow the response, which matters in interactive applications such as communication aids. Knowledge distillation addresses this challenge by training a smaller or faster student model to imitate a more computationally expensive teacher. \textcite{li2024dmospeech} developed DMOSpeech, a zero-shot TTS system that uses distribution-matching distillation and reduces the generation process from 128 steps to four. Similarly, \textcite{zhu2025zipvoice} introduced ZipVoice, a flow-matching TTS system with a distilled version designed to speed up speech generation. Within flow-matching TTS, \textcite{zheng2025epss} identified redundant sampling steps in F5-TTS and removed them without retraining, although the same pruned schedule is applied to every input. The cost is also higher than the step count alone suggests, because classifier-free guidance requires two forward passes at each step \parencite{ho2022cfg,yang2025shallow}. These studies show that distillation and coarser step schedules can lower the computing needs of speech generation without retraining the networks. However, a schedule shortened in the same way for every input does not show that the shorter path keeps speech quality for every input, in particular when the recording of the speaker is noisy.

Building on this direction, \textcite{li2025dmospeech2} developed DMOSpeech~2, a teacher-guided TTS system in which a teacher model carries out the first generation steps and a distilled student model completes the speech. In this approach, the teacher runs fewer steps than in its full generation process, approximately 6 to 14 instead of 32 \parencite[p.~31818]{li2025dmospeech2}. The authors report that this approach improves output diversity compared with using the student alone, while remaining more efficient than running the full teacher. This provides a direct precedent for combining teacher guidance and distilled generation in TTS. At the handover, the teacher does not pass its weights or a reduced set of features to the student; it passes its current estimate of the final mel-spectrogram, which it computes from its last step at no extra cost \parencite{li2025dmospeech2code,lipman2024guide}. This estimate is the teacher's \emph{payload}. However, the handover is the same for every utterance in two respects. The teacher always takes the same number of steps on the same fine time grid before it stops, which in the released code costs 18 of the 21 forward passes of an utterance \parencite{li2025dmospeech2code}; and the student always receives the teacher's own estimate, whatever the condition of the speaker prompt. Because the teacher continues the prompt recording, its estimate can carry the prompt's background noise or channel effects into the speech that the student finishes. The team's preliminary experiments (Appendices~\ref{app:prelim} and~\ref{app:snr-r}) found that cheaper handovers added quality failures on noisy prompts, and that a check of the prompt's noise level alone missed some kinds of damaged prompts. Studying whether the teacher can reach the same handover point in fewer steps, and whether \emph{what} it hands over can be chosen separately for each utterance, may therefore offer a way to lower the computing effort for every input while protecting quality where the prompt is damaged, keeping the published generation procedure of the distilled student.

Adjusting generation separately for each input has been studied in other generative tasks. In flow-matching image and video generation, \textcite{bajpai2026fastflow} and \textcite{bajpai2026coflow} choose sampling steps for each input without retraining, noting that \enquote{a fixed step budget may be unnecessarily conservative for some inputs while insufficient for others} \parencite[p.~3]{bajpai2026coflow}. \textcite{zhang2025adadiff} likewise select a step count for each prompt in diffusion models, although their selection policy must be trained. Closer to a teacher--student setting, \textcite{starodubcev2024your} proposed a method for generating images from text in which a student first creates an image and a quality estimator decides whether the teacher needs to refine it further. This approach shows how a cheap first attempt by the student can inform a decision about the teacher's contribution. \textcite{mualem2026aria} applied a related idea during training rather than generation, giving more training samples to groups of prompts where the student differs most from the teacher. In speech, \textcite{hsieh2025adflowtse} allocate more flow-matching steps to noisier inputs in target speaker extraction, although their estimate of the noise level comes from a trained predictor. Closest in structure, \textcite{cheng2025srdiffusion} ran a large video-generation model for the early, high-noise steps and handed over to a smaller model at a step chosen for each prompt, based on when the change in the generated sample between steps had levelled off; the switching threshold, however, was set by hand, and the smaller model was not a distilled student. These studies show that computation, including the point at which a large model hands over to a smaller one, can be assigned according to the input. However, all of them adapt \emph{how much} computation an input receives or \emph{when} the handover happens; none decides \emph{what} a teacher passes to a distilled student, and none addresses speech synthesis. The reviewed sources therefore provide related ideas but do not establish a method for choosing the teacher's payload for individual utterances in flow-matching TTS.

A per-utterance choice of payload also raises the question of how reliable its decisions are. Choosing between payloads based on signals available at inference does not, by itself, show how often the choice may introduce quality failures that the released system would not have made. Statistical risk control has already been applied to adaptive computation: \textcite{schuster2022calm} calibrated early exiting in language models, \textcite{jazbec2024fast} applied risk control to early exiting in diffusion-based image generation, and \textcite{damri2026confident} used Learn-Then-Test to choose, for each input, how many recognition hypotheses to pass to a language-model corrector in speech recognition. \textcite{wu2025thought} likewise used Learn-Then-Test to decide when a language model can stop reasoning. These studies show that statistically calibrated decisions at inference are feasible, but they address text, images, or speech recognition rather than speech synthesis. None of the adaptive handover methods described above attaches such a guarantee to its handover decision. Learn-Then-Test (LTT), introduced by \textcite{angelopoulos2025ltt}, is a statistical framework for choosing among a limited set of candidate settings while making sure, at a chosen confidence level, that the risk of the chosen setting does not go beyond a stated limit, as long as the framework's assumptions are met. Applying LTT to teacher-guided TTS requires defining what counts as a quality failure, choosing a reference system, stating the acceptable risk limit, and using suitable calibration data. In this study, LTT will be used to control the measured rate of quality failures added relative to the released system, not to guarantee the quality of every individual utterance.

The reviewed literature provides the foundations for this study through flow-matching TTS, distilled speech generation, teacher-guided sampling, adaptive computation, and statistical risk calibration. However, within the sources examined, adaptive handovers from a large to a smaller model have been decided per input only with thresholds set by hand, without a statistical guarantee on the quality failures they add; they have changed when the handover happens, not what is handed over; and none has been applied to text-to-speech. No method has therefore been established that chooses, for each utterance, what the teacher hands to the student and statistically bounds the added risk of quality failures in flow-matching TTS. Related adaptive and risk-controlled methods exist for image and video generation, language models, and speech recognition, so this gap is limited to this specific combination in the literature reviewed and does not mean that no other adaptive methods exist. The study will therefore investigate whether a per-utterance choice of payload can keep speech quality at a lower, fixed teacher cost while keeping the added quality failures at a statistically controlled level.

To address this problem, the study will develop a training-free, risk-calibrated \emph{payload router} that uses the distilled model of DMOSpeech~2 by \textcite{li2025dmospeech2}. The teacher will stop at the same point as in the released system, the \emph{commit point}, but will reach it in four steps of a coarser time grid instead of eight, so every utterance will cost 13 forward passes instead of the released system's 21. At the commit point, the router will choose between two payloads: the teacher's own estimate of the final mel-spectrogram, which carries its full detail and variety of intonation, and a corrected version called the \emph{quantile projection}, which keeps the teacher's timing but takes its loudness values, band by band, from a one-step draft made by the student. The choice will rest on two signals available at inference that need no extra network evaluation: the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the speaker prompt, and the \emph{invariant residual}, which measures how far the teacher's estimate is from its projection, for example because the teacher has copied the prompt's background noise. The router will pass the projection when the prompt seems noisy or the residual is above a threshold, and the teacher's estimate otherwise. The student will then finish the speech in two steps from the chosen payload, starting at the level where the teacher stopped, and all pretrained model weights will be kept unchanged. LTT will be used to set the router's single threshold once, on a separate group of speakers, so that the rate of quality failures added relative to the released system stays within a predefined limit, subject to the framework's statistical assumptions. The method will be evaluated using word error rate (WER), speaker similarity (SIM), predicted naturalness (UTMOS), pitch variety across random seeds, neural function evaluations (NFEs) or forward-pass counts, and runtime. These measures will be used to determine whether the payload router can reduce computing cost while controlling the measured risk of added quality failures.

Efficient and responsive speech generation may also be relevant to assistive information and communication technology for autistic users. Research on augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), including speech-generating devices, has looked at their role in supporting communication among autistic individuals \parencite{muharib2024sgd,therrien2025sgd}. A TTS component can provide spoken output for text that a user selects or types, which makes the efficiency of speech generation relevant to communication-support applications. Voice prompts recorded in everyday settings are also often noisy, which makes robustness to the condition of the prompt relevant to such uses. In this study, the assistive-technology context of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) motivates the practical value of developing a more computationally efficient TTS method. The main evaluation, however, will focus on speech quality, computing cost, and the statistical control of added quality failures. It will not assess clinical outcomes or establish how effective the resulting application is as an AAC intervention. Experiments will use an NVIDIA Tesla T4 graphics processing unit (GPU) through Google Colab, while use on mobile or other devices with limited computing power remains a possible direction for future work.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Speaking without delay matters especially when a person relies on a device to communicate. DMOSpeech~2, developed by \textcite{li2025dmospeech2}, produces speech by letting a teacher model carry out the first generation steps and a distilled student model finish the speech. Every utterance receives the same amount of teacher computing and the same kind of handover, whatever the condition of its speaker prompt. However, this fixed teacher cost may be more than the handover needs, and a handover that ignores the prompt's condition may lose quality exactly when the prompt is noisy. Lowering the cost is useful only if the quality it may lose is controlled. These concerns matter for efficient text-to-speech (TTS), including its possible use in communication-support applications, such as those intended for autistic users.

To begin with, the teacher's share of the computation is fixed and large. In the released implementation of DMOSpeech~2, the switching time of 0.25 stops the teacher at step~8 of its 16-step time grid, so every utterance costs 21 forward passes, 18 of them spent by the teacher, because each guided teacher step requires two forward passes \parencite{li2025dmospeech2code,ho2022cfg,yang2025shallow}. Flow-matching paths are close to straight \parencite{liu2023flow}, and redundant steps of F5-TTS have been removed without retraining \parencite{zheng2025epss}, which suggests that the teacher may reach the same handover point in fewer, larger steps. Reaching it in four steps of an 8-step grid would cost 13 forward passes, 38\% fewer than the released sampler. DMOSpeech~2 reports a single teacher-guided setting, with results averaged over its test sets \parencite[p.~31819]{li2025dmospeech2}, so whether such a shorter teacher path keeps intelligibility, speaker similarity, predicted naturalness and variety of intonation needs to be tested through experiments.

Building on this, the content of the handover is also fixed. In the released implementation, the student takes the teacher's estimate of the final result and combines it with fresh noise according to its restart setting \parencite{li2025dmospeech2code}. Because the teacher continues the prompt recording, a noisy or otherwise damaged prompt can leave its traces in that estimate, and the student finishes from it. The team's preliminary experiments (Appendix~\ref{app:prelim}) found that cheaper handovers added quality failures on noisy prompts, and that a decision based only on the prompt's signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) missed prompts damaged in ways that the SNR does not show (Appendix~\ref{app:snr-r}). Replacing the teacher's estimate for every input would protect against damaged prompts but would also discard the variety of intonation that teacher guidance was designed to add \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}. It is still unclear whether a rule that chooses the payload for each utterance, using signals available at inference and without extra forward passes, can keep the teacher's estimate where it is safe and correct it where it is not.

For such a choice to be usable, the quality it may lose must be known before use. A routing threshold tuned on data does not, by itself, show how often the rule will fail where the released system does not. Statistical risk control has been applied to adaptive computation in other tasks, such as language models, image generation, and speech recognition \parencite{damri2026confident,jazbec2024fast,schuster2022calm,wu2025thought}, but within the sources reviewed, not to the teacher-to-student handover in TTS. Learn-Then-Test provides a framework for setting a decision threshold among candidate settings so that the risk stays within a stated limit, as long as its assumptions are met \parencite{angelopoulos2025ltt}. A payload router therefore needs its threshold to be calibrated in this way, using a defined quality-failure criterion, calibration data kept separate from the evaluation, and a risk limit set beforehand.

Finally, these technical concerns connect to the use of speech synthesis for autistic users. Research has examined speech-generating devices as communication-support tools for autistic individuals \parencite{muharib2024sgd,therrien2025sgd}. However, the possible relevance of TTS to these uses does not show that a more computationally efficient method improves communication outcomes. The technical performance of the proposed method must therefore be evaluated without assuming that it is usable or effective as an assistive intervention.

In view of these concerns, the general problem addressed by this study is whether a training-free, risk-calibrated payload router can reduce the computing cost of teacher-guided flow-matching TTS to a fixed, lower number of forward passes while keeping the added risk of quality failures, compared with the released system, within a limit set beforehand. Specifically, the study seeks to answer the following research questions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item How do the released DMOSpeech~2 system, the other established systems, and the two payloads at the shorter teacher path (the teacher's estimate and its quantile projection) perform in speech quality (intelligibility, speaker similarity, and predicted naturalness), pitch variety, forward passes and runtime under clean and degraded prompt conditions, and how do the prompt SNR and the invariant residual relate to the cases in which the teacher's estimate fails and the projection does not?
\item Can a training-free rule that chooses one of the two payloads for each utterance, using the prompt SNR and the invariant residual, with its single threshold calibrated by Learn-Then-Test, keep the risk of quality failures added relative to the released system within a limit set beforehand, at a fixed cost of 13 forward passes, without additional network evaluations for its signals or changes to the model weights?
\item How does the calibrated router compare with the established systems and with simpler rules (always projecting, never projecting, a rule based only on the prompt SNR, other commit points, and the student restarted from its own draft) in speech quality, pitch variety, computing cost, and the rate of added quality failures under clean and degraded prompt conditions; does the observed rate on held-out speakers and on a second dataset stay within the limit; does the invariant residual add value beyond the prompt SNR; and how do the threshold and the guarantee vary with calibration sample size and with a shift from clean to degraded prompts?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Objectives of the Study}
\label{sec:objectives}

\subsubsection{General Objective}
The general objective of this study is to develop and evaluate a training-free, risk-calibrated payload router for teacher-guided flow-matching text-to-speech (TTS) that stops the teacher at a fixed commit point reached in fewer steps and chooses, for each utterance, whether the student receives the teacher's estimate of the speech or its quantile projection, aiming to lower the computing cost of every utterance below that of the released DMOSpeech~2 system while keeping the rate of added quality failures, compared with that system, within a risk limit set beforehand, with all model weights kept unchanged.

\subsubsection{Specific Objectives}
Specifically, this study aims to:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Characterize the baseline and the handover payloads.} Reproduce the released DMOSpeech~2 system and the other established systems, and measure how word error rate (WER), speaker similarity (SIM), predicted naturalness (UTMOS), pitch variety, forward-pass counts, and runtime differ between the teacher's estimate and its quantile projection at the shorter teacher path, under clean and degraded prompt conditions; fix the minimum quality levels of the failure rule; and record how the prompt signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and the invariant residual relate to failures of each payload.
\item \textbf{Develop the payload router.} Define the choice of payload for each utterance as a problem of passing the teacher's estimate to as many utterances as possible while keeping the risk of added quality failures within a set limit; define the quantile projection, the invariant residual and the routing rule without changing the pretrained model weights and without additional network evaluations for the signals; analyze the properties of the resulting method, including its cost; and calibrate the router's threshold with Learn-Then-Test (LTT) on a separate group of speakers so that this risk, compared with the released system, stays within a limit and confidence level set beforehand, falling back to the released system if no threshold passes.
\item \textbf{Evaluate the method and test its robustness.} Check whether the observed rate of added quality failures on held-out speakers and on a second, unseen dataset stays within the set limit; compare the calibrated router with the established systems and with the ablations (always projecting, never projecting, a rule based only on the prompt SNR, other commit points, and the student restarted from its own draft) using WER, SIM, UTMOS, pitch variety, the rate of added quality failures, forward-pass counts, and runtime under clean and degraded prompt conditions; determine whether the invariant residual adds value beyond the prompt SNR; and examine how the threshold and the reliability guarantee vary with calibration sample size, the risk limit, and a shift from clean to degraded prompts.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Significance of the Study}
\label{sec:significance}

This study investigates a training-free, risk-calibrated payload router for teacher-guided flow-matching text-to-speech (TTS). Its findings may contribute to more efficient speech synthesis that is more robust to noisy speaker prompts, while keeping the rate of added quality failures within a risk limit set beforehand. The results are expected to benefit the following:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=2pt,font=\normalfont\itshape]
\item[TTS researchers.] This study may provide insights into how much of the teacher's computation is needed before the handover, how the content of the handover affects intelligibility, speaker similarity, naturalness and variety of intonation, and how this depends on the condition of the speaker prompt, supporting further research on teacher-guided TTS.
\item[TTS developers.] The proposed router may offer a way to lower the cost of every utterance to a fixed, known number of forward passes without retraining the pretrained models and without additional network evaluations for its decision, and to protect quality when users supply noisy prompts. Because the method falls back to the released system whenever no threshold passes calibration, it is designed never to cost more than the original sampler.
\item[Researchers in reliable machine learning.] The application of Learn-Then-Test (LTT) may demonstrate how statistical risk control can be used to calibrate a decision made during generation in a generative model, under stated calibration assumptions.
\item[Assistive technology and AAC developers.] The findings may inform future research on efficient and noise-robust TTS components for communication-support technologies, including augmentative and alternative communication (AAC).
\item[Autistic and neurodivergent learners.] The study may indirectly support future assistive-technology research by examining the technical performance of speech synthesis. However, it does not directly evaluate usability or communication outcomes for these learners.
\item[Future researchers.] The methodology and findings may serve as a reference for further studies on handover design, risk-calibrated inference, and efficient TTS across different datasets, noise conditions, and hardware, including a calibrated threshold, reported with the conditions under which it holds.
\end{description}

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:scope}

This study will focus on developing and evaluating a training-free, risk-calibrated method for choosing, for each utterance, what the teacher hands to the student in teacher-guided flow-matching text-to-speech (TTS), using DMOSpeech~2 and its F5-TTS teacher \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}. The handover will be adaptive in its content, not in its amount of computation: every utterance will cost the same number of forward passes, and the method will decide for each utterance whether to pass the teacher's own estimate of the output or its quantile projection. The study will cover the shorter teacher path, two decision signals available during generation (the prompt signal-to-noise ratio and the invariant residual between the teacher's estimate and a one-step student draft), the routing rule, statistical calibration with Learn-Then-Test, and objective evaluation of speech quality, prosodic variety and computing cost. Because only one teacher--student system will be studied, the findings may not transfer to other teacher-guided TTS systems.

The teacher will stop at one fixed point for every utterance, the handover point of the released DMOSpeech~2 code (switching time 0.25), reached in four steps of a coarser grid instead of eight, so the method will cost 13 forward passes against the released sampler's 21. Two other stopping points, one earlier and one later, will be tested as ablations, but the stopping point will not be chosen for each utterance; making the amount of computation itself depend on the input is outside the scope of this study. The per-utterance switching-time scheduler studied in the team's preliminary experiments is reported only as development evidence (Appendix~\ref{app:prelim}).

All pretrained model weights, including those of the teacher, the student, the duration predictor, and the vocoder, will be kept unchanged. Duration prediction will follow the published procedure and will not be part of the proposed contribution. The student will finish in two steps, restarting at the level where the teacher stops, which is also the two-step configuration of the DMOSpeech~2 paper.

The evaluation will use English zero-shot prompts from two test sets: the Seed-TTS test set, which draws on Common Voice \parencite{anastassiou2024seedtts,ardila2020commonvoice}, and the test-clean portion of LibriSpeech-PC, read audiobook speech from LibriSpeech with restored punctuation and capitalization \parencite{panayotov2015librispeech,meister2023librispeechpc}, under clean and degraded prompt conditions. Calibration data will be kept separate from evaluation data, and LibriSpeech-PC will be used only for testing. Therefore, the findings may not apply to other languages, other recording conditions, or very different inputs. Speech quality will be measured only with automatic measures: word error rate (WER), speaker similarity (SIM), predicted naturalness (UTMOS), and a pitch-based measure of prosodic variety across random seeds, the property that teacher-guided sampling was designed to improve. The method will not be compared with step-pruning methods that shorten the teacher's own schedule for every input, such as the empirically pruned step sampling of \textcite{zheng2025epss}; the study's results will be stated relative to the released teacher-guided sampler rather than as the lowest possible cost of synthesis.

The reliability guarantee will hold only under the statistical assumptions of the calibration procedure. It will limit the added risk of quality failures on average across inputs drawn from the same distribution as the calibration data, and it will not guarantee acceptable quality for every individual utterance. The guarantee may not hold when the inputs differ from the calibration data. In addition, running time will be measured only on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU through Google Colab, and no measurements will be made on mobile or other limited-resource devices. Furthermore, the study will evaluate only technical performance and computing efficiency. It will not assess clinical effectiveness, user experience, communication outcomes, or actual use in mobile assistive technologies. Listening tests with people will also be outside the scope of the study, except for a possible small diagnostic check by the research team.

\subsection{Definition of Terms}
\label{sec:terms}

The following terms are defined as they are used in this study.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=2pt,font=\normalfont\itshape]
\item[Added quality failure.] An utterance on which the router's output fails the quality-failure rule while the released system's output for the same input passes it; also called an excess failure.
\item[Calibration.] Choosing the router's threshold on a separate group of speakers, not used for the final evaluation, so that the risk is bounded with a stated confidence.
\item[Commit point.] The fixed point at which the teacher stops and hands over to the student. In this study it is the same point as in the released system ($t\approx0.293$), reached in four steps of an 8-step grid instead of eight steps of a 16-step grid.
\item[Endpoint estimate.] The teacher's prediction of the final mel-spectrogram, computed at any step from the current state and velocity without an extra network evaluation; also called the teacher payload. No weights, gradients or separate features are passed.
\item[Flow matching.] A way of generating data in which a network learns the direction in which random noise should move, step by step, to become speech.
\item[Forward pass.] One evaluation of a neural network, also called a neural function evaluation. Computing cost is counted in forward passes, with a guided teacher step counting as two.
\item[Invariant residual.] The size of the difference between the teacher's endpoint estimate and its quantile projection, relative to the size of the estimate. A large residual means the teacher's loudness values have moved far from the student draft's, for example because the teacher copied the prompt's background noise.
\item[Learn-Then-Test.] The statistical procedure used to choose the router's threshold so that the risk stays below the tolerance with a stated confidence, under its assumptions.
\item[Mel-spectrogram.] A picture of sound that shows how much energy each frequency band has in each short time frame; both networks generate it, and the vocoder turns it into audio.
\item[Payload.] What the teacher hands to the student at the commit point: either its endpoint estimate or the quantile projection of that estimate.
\item[Payload router.] The rule that chooses the payload for each utterance: it passes the quantile projection when the prompt SNR is below a fixed level or the invariant residual is above the calibrated threshold, and the endpoint estimate otherwise.
\item[Pitch variety.] How much the melody of the generated speech for the same input changes across random seeds; the property that teacher-guided sampling was designed to improve.
\item[Prompt.] The short recording of a speaker given to the system, whose voice the generated speech should match.
\item[Prompt condition.] The state of the prompt recording: clean, or degraded by one of the added kinds of noise or channel effects used in the evaluation.
\item[Prompt SNR.] An estimate, in decibels, of how loud the speech in the prompt is compared with its background noise.
\item[Quality failure.] An output whose word error rate is above a fixed ceiling or whose speaker similarity or UTMOS is below a minimum level, under a rule fixed before calibration.
\item[Quantile projection.] A corrected payload that keeps, in each frequency band, the order of the teacher's values over time, and therefore its timing, but replaces the values themselves with those of the student draft, sorted in the same order.
\item[Released system.] DMOSpeech~2 run as in its released code, with the teacher stopping after eight guided steps (21 forward passes); the reference against which the cost and added failures of every other system are measured.
\item[Reliability guarantee.] The statement produced by calibration that, with probability at least $1-\delta$, the risk of the chosen threshold is at most $\alpha$; it holds on average over inputs, not for each utterance.
\item[Restart level.] The point on the student's own schedule at which it starts generating from the payload mixed with fresh noise; in this study the student finishes in two steps from the level at which the teacher stopped.
\item[Risk.] The expected rate of added quality failures over new inputs.
\item[Routing rate.] The share of utterances that receive the quantile projection instead of the endpoint estimate.
\item[Speaker similarity.] How closely the voice in the generated speech matches the reference recording, measured by a speaker-verification model.
\item[Student draft.] The student's one-step prediction of the whole output directly from noise, costing one forward pass; the quantile projection takes its values from it.
\item[Student model.] The smaller, distilled model that finishes generation in a few steps; in this study, the DMOSpeech~2 generator.
\item[Switching time.] The point in generation at which the teacher stops and the student takes over; also called the handover point. In this study it is fixed at the commit point.
\item[Teacher model.] The larger, multi-step model whose outputs the student was trained to imitate; in this study, F5-TTS.
\item[Teacher-guided sampling.] Generation in which the teacher performs the first steps and the student completes the output.
\item[Training-free.] Describes a method that changes only how the released models are run and does not train or fine-tune any network.
\item[UTMOS.] A model that predicts the mean opinion score listeners would give an utterance; it is used as an estimate of naturalness.
\item[Velocity.] The direction and rate in which the teacher moves the mel-spectrogram at a given step. It is the network's output, not the gradient of a training loss, and the endpoint estimate is computed from it.
\item[Vocoder.] The network that turns the final mel-spectrogram into a waveform; in this study, Vocos.
\item[Word error rate.] The share of words that a speech recognizer gets wrong when it transcribes the generated speech, compared with the input text; it measures intelligibility.
\item[Zero-shot TTS.] Speech generation in the voice of a speaker heard only through a short prompt, without training the model on that speaker.
\end{description}
