\subsection{Background of the Study}
\label{sec:background}

Giving computers a voice is the goal of text-to-speech (TTS) technology, which turns written text into spoken output while aiming to keep the speech clear, true to the speaker's voice, and natural. One approach used in generative TTS systems is flow matching, which describes a smooth change from random noise into a representation of speech. In flow-matching TTS, the model learns a path from noise to speech and follows it through a series of calculations to produce speech \parencite{lipman2023flow,liu2023flow}. F5-TTS, developed by \textcite{chen2024f5}, uses flow matching through a Diffusion Transformer and introduces Sway Sampling, which places more of the solver's steps early in generation, where the overall structure of the speech is decided. These developments form a basis for studying ways to generate speech more efficiently, especially because a model's full computing cost may not be needed for every input.

Despite these advances, generating speech can still require many repeated calculations by the neural network. This increases computing cost and may slow the response, which matters in interactive applications such as communication aids. Knowledge distillation addresses this by training a faster student model to imitate a more computationally expensive teacher. \textcite{li2024dmospeech} developed DMOSpeech, a zero-shot TTS system that uses distribution-matching distillation and reduces the generation process from 128 steps to four. Within flow-matching TTS, \textcite{zheng2025epss} identified redundant sampling steps in F5-TTS and removed them without retraining, although the same pruned schedule is applied to every input. The cost is also higher than the step count alone suggests, because classifier-free guidance requires two forward passes at each step \parencite{ho2022cfg,yang2025shallow}. Distilled students also tend to lose some of the variety in intonation that the teacher produces \parencite[pp.~31817--31818]{li2025dmospeech2}.

Building in this direction, \textcite{li2025dmospeech2} developed DMOSpeech~2, a teacher-guided TTS system in which a teacher model carries out the first generation steps and a distilled student model completes the speech. In this approach, the teacher runs fewer steps than in its full generation process, approximately 6 to 14 instead of 32 \parencite[p.~31818]{li2025dmospeech2}. In the released system, the teacher always runs the same number of steps and always hands the student the same thing: its own estimate of the final speech \parencite{li2025dmospeech2code}. Results are reported as averages over clean test sets \parencite[p.~31819]{li2025dmospeech2}, so it is not known how this handover behaves when the speaker's recording, called the prompt, is noisy or otherwise degraded. Zero-shot TTS systems are known to copy the conditions of the prompt: zero-shot models that learn the speaker's voice from the audio prompt \enquote{tend to preserve the environmental characteristics of the audio prompt in the synthesized speech} \parencite[p.~1]{lu2026environment}. The remedies proposed so far retrain the model \parencite{lu2026environment,wang2024noise}, and cleaning the prompt beforehand is not enough, because applying speech enhancement to the prompt \enquote{causes degradation in both intelligibility and speaker characteristics of the generated audio} \parencite[p.~1]{wang2024noise}. In teacher-guided sampling, the teacher's estimate is computed with the prompt as a guide, so it may pass these conditions on to the student, and a teacher that takes fewer steps to save computation has had less opportunity to settle the speech. A cheaper teacher path is therefore possible only if what the teacher hands over can be protected on such prompts.

Adjusting computation separately for each input has been studied in other generative tasks. In flow-matching image and video generation, \textcite{bajpai2026fastflow} and \textcite{bajpai2026coflow} choose sampling steps for each input without retraining. Closer to a teacher--student setting, \textcite{starodubcev2024your} let a quality estimator decide whether the teacher should further refine an image that the student generated. Closest in structure, \textcite{cheng2025srdiffusion} ran a large video-generation model for the early, high-noise steps and handed over to a smaller model at a step chosen for each prompt; the switching threshold, however, was set by hand, and the smaller model was not a distilled student. Recent large-to-small methods also use the disagreement between the two models as a signal, but average it offline or set its threshold by hand \parencite{munir2026track,sun2026hybridstitch}. All of these studies adapt how much computation is spent or when the handover happens; none adapts what is handed over, and none addresses speech synthesis.

A separate line of work suggests what a protected handover could look like. In noise-robust speech recognition, histogram equalization removes the mismatch that background noise causes in speech features by mapping each feature's values onto a reference distribution while keeping their order in time \parencite{delatorre2005histogram,dharanipragada2000nonlinear}. The same operation can be applied to the teacher's estimate: it would keep when each sound rises and falls, which carries the teacher's timing and intonation, while taking the loudness values from a quick first attempt by the student, which works from noise and does not copy the prompt's recording conditions in the same way. Such a correction removes some of the teacher's detail, so it should be applied only when the teacher's estimate appears to be affected.

An adaptive handover also raises the question of how reliable its decisions are. Statistical risk control has already been applied to adaptive computation, including early exiting in language models and image generation, and the number of recognition hypotheses passed to a corrector in speech recognition \parencite{damri2026confident,jazbec2024fast,schuster2022calm}. Calibrated choices between a safe default and a riskier alternative have also been made outside speech: \textcite{xie2026lowlight} override a fixed image-enhancement model with a generative one only when a calibrated certificate allows it, and otherwise return \enquote{the fixed base expert} (p.~2). Learn-Then-Test (LTT), proposed by \textcite{angelopoulos2025ltt}, selects among a limited set of candidate settings so that, at a chosen confidence level and under the framework's assumptions, the risk of the chosen setting does not go beyond a stated limit. In this study, LTT will be used to control the rate at which the proposed method fails where the released system does not, not to guarantee the quality of every individual utterance.

Within the sources reviewed, no method has been established that chooses, for each utterance, what a teacher hands to a distilled student and statistically bounds the rate of excess failures, that is, failures that the released system does not have.

To address this problem, the study will develop a training-free, risk-calibrated payload router for DMOSpeech~2 \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}. The teacher will take fewer steps than in the released system. Several shorter teacher paths, each costing fewer forward passes than the released system's 21, will be considered as candidates; each will be calibrated and evaluated, and on any one path every utterance costs the same number of forward passes. The student will also make a one-step first attempt from noise, called the draft. For each utterance, a router will then decide what the teacher hands to the student, called the payload: either the teacher's own estimate, or a corrected version that keeps the teacher's timing but takes its loudness values from the draft. The router will use two signals that need no network evaluation beyond the draft: the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the prompt, and a residual that measures how far the teacher's loudness values have moved from the draft's. LTT will be used to calibrate, for each path, the router's threshold so that the rate of excess failures stays within a predefined limit; a path for which no threshold passes uses the released system unchanged. Comparing the calibrated paths will show which one is least costly while staying within that limit. The method will be evaluated on clean and degraded prompts in terms of speech quality, prosodic variety, and computing cost.

Efficient and responsive speech generation may also be relevant to assistive information and communication technology for autistic users. Research on augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), including speech-generating devices, has looked at their role in supporting communication among autistic individuals \parencite{muharib2024sgd,therrien2025sgd}. A TTS component can provide spoken output for text that a user selects or types, which makes the efficiency of speech generation relevant to communication-support applications, and a speaker's reference recording may not be clean. Clinical, usability, and on-device questions are outside the scope of this study (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).

\subsection{Conceptual Framework}
\label{sec:conceptual}

Figure~\ref{fig:conceptual} shows the conceptual framework as input, process and output. The inputs are a speaker prompt, clean or noisy, with the text to be spoken, and a separate group of speakers used only for calibration. The process is the released teacher--student system of DMOSpeech~2 \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}, kept unchanged except for a shorter teacher path and the handover. There, the proposed method decides for each utterance whether the teacher passes its own estimate of the speech or a version corrected for prompt noise, guided by two signals. The router's threshold is set once for each candidate teacher path, by calibration, so that the method fails where the released system does not on no more than a stated share of inputs. The output is the synthesized speech, produced at a fixed cost lower than that of the released system. Chapter~3 describes each component (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}).

\subsection{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Speaking without delay matters especially when a person relies on a device to communicate. DMOSpeech~2, developed by \textcite{li2025dmospeech2}, produces speech by letting a teacher model carry out the first generation steps and a distilled student model finish the speech. In the released system, the teacher always runs the same number of steps and always hands the student its own estimate of the final speech. Running the teacher for fewer steps would lower computing cost, but what the teacher hands over may then carry problems from the speaker's recording into the speech, and any rule that protects against this raises questions about how reliable its decisions are.

To begin with, it is not known how the released system and a shortened teacher behave when the speaker's recording, the prompt, is degraded. DMOSpeech~2 reports a single teacher-guided setting, with results averaged over clean test sets \parencite[p.~31819]{li2025dmospeech2}. Because each guided teacher step requires two forward passes \parencite{ho2022cfg,yang2025shallow}, the teacher accounts for most of the cost, so a shorter teacher path is the obvious place to save computation. However, zero-shot TTS models are known to copy noise and other conditions of the prompt into the speech they generate \parencite{lu2026environment,wang2024noise}, and in teacher-guided sampling the teacher's estimate may pass these conditions on to the student, particularly when the teacher takes fewer steps. How the released system and the two possible handover contents, the teacher's estimate and a corrected version of it, at different teacher path lengths, perform under clean and degraded prompts therefore needs to be measured, in terms of intelligibility, speaker similarity, predicted naturalness, and cost.

Building on this, the content of the handover itself is a concern. A corrected version that keeps the teacher's timing but replaces its loudness values could remove copied noise, but it also removes part of the teacher's detail, including some of the variety in intonation that teacher guidance was introduced to restore \parencite[p.~31818]{li2025dmospeech2}. Correcting every input would give up that detail on clean prompts, while never correcting would leave degraded prompts unprotected. It is still unclear whether the choice between the two can be made for each utterance, from signals available during generation and without changes to the model weights.

Beyond these effects on quality and cost, there is no established measure of reliability for such a choice. Statistical risk control has been applied to adaptive computation in other tasks \parencite{damri2026confident,jazbec2024fast,schuster2022calm}, but within the sources reviewed, not to the teacher-to-student handover in TTS. Whether Learn-Then-Test \parencite{angelopoulos2025ltt} can bound the rate at which an adaptive handover fails where the released system does not must therefore be examined.

Finally, these technical concerns connect to the use of speech synthesis for autistic users. However, the possible relevance of TTS to these uses does not show that a more computationally efficient method improves communication outcomes. The technical performance of the proposed method must therefore be evaluated without assuming that it is usable or effective as an assistive intervention.

In view of these concerns, the general problem addressed by this study is whether a risk-calibrated method that chooses, for each utterance, what the teacher hands to the student can reduce the computing cost of teacher-guided flow-matching TTS while keeping the rate of excess failures, compared with the released system, within a limit set beforehand. Specifically, the study seeks to answer the following research questions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item How do the released DMOSpeech~2 system and the two handover contents of shortened teacher paths, the teacher's own estimate and a corrected version of it, perform in speech quality (intelligibility, speaker similarity, and predicted naturalness) and computing cost under clean and degraded prompts?
\item Can the choice between the two handover contents be made for each utterance from signals available during generation, without changes to the model weights, and can the Learn-Then-Test framework calibrate, for each candidate teacher path, a threshold for which the rate of excess failures relative to the released system stays within a limit set beforehand?
\item How do the calibrated teacher paths compare with one another and with the released system, the other established configurations, and simpler versions of the method in computing cost, speech quality, prosodic variety, and the rate of excess failures, and how well does the guarantee hold across prompt conditions, calibration sample sizes, and a second dataset?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Objectives of the Study}
\label{sec:objectives}

\subsubsection{General Objective}
The general objective of this study is to develop and evaluate a training-free, risk-calibrated payload router that chooses, for each utterance, what the teacher hands to the student in teacher-guided flow-matching text-to-speech (TTS), so that every utterance costs fewer forward passes than the released system while the rate of excess failures, compared with that system, stays within a risk limit set beforehand, with all model weights kept unchanged.

\subsubsection{Specific Objectives}
Specifically, this study aims to:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item Reproduce the released DMOSpeech~2 system and the other established configurations, and measure word error rate (WER), speaker similarity (SIM), predicted naturalness (UTMOS), forward-pass counts, and runtime for the released system and for the two payloads of each candidate teacher path, the teacher's estimate and its corrected version, under clean and degraded prompt conditions.
\item Formulate the choice between the two payloads for each utterance as a problem of keeping the teacher's estimate as often as a set risk limit allows; define the corrected payload, the residual signal, and the routing rule, and prove their properties; and calibrate the router's threshold for each candidate teacher path with Learn-Then-Test (LTT) so that the rate of excess failures relative to the released system stays within a limit and confidence level set beforehand.
\item Evaluate the method and test its robustness on speakers not used to develop or calibrate the method and on a second dataset: compare the router on each candidate teacher path with the released system, the other established configurations, and simpler versions of the router, and identify the least costly path that stays within the limit, using forward-pass counts, runtime, WER, SIM, UTMOS, prosodic variety, failure rates, and the rate of excess failures; check whether the observed rate of excess failures stays within the calibrated limit; and examine how the results vary across prompt conditions, calibration sample sizes, and the tolerated risk.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Significance of the Study}
\label{sec:significance}

This study investigates a training-free, risk-calibrated payload router for teacher-guided flow-matching text-to-speech (TTS). Its findings may contribute to more efficient speech synthesis while keeping the rate of excess failures within a risk limit set beforehand. The results are expected to benefit the following:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=2pt,font=\normalfont\itshape]
\item[TTS researchers.] This study may provide insights into how degraded speaker recordings affect teacher-guided generation, whether the teacher's estimate carries recording problems into the speech, and how much of the teacher's work is needed for quality and prosodic variety, supporting further research on efficient TTS inference.
\item[TTS developers.] The proposed method may offer a way to run teacher-guided TTS at a fixed, lower cost per utterance than the released system, without retraining the pretrained models, while controlling the rate of excess failures.
\item[Researchers in reliable machine learning.] The application of Learn-Then-Test (LTT) may demonstrate how statistical risk control can be used to calibrate a decision inside a generative model, with a bound stated against the failure rate of an existing system, under stated calibration assumptions.
\item[Assistive technology and AAC developers.] The findings may inform future research on efficient TTS components for communication-support technologies, including augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), where speaker recordings may not be clean.
\item[Autistic and neurodivergent learners.] The study may indirectly support future assistive-technology research by examining the technical performance of speech synthesis.
\item[Future researchers.] The methodology, the calibrated thresholds, the comparison of teacher paths, and the conditions under which they hold may serve as a reference for further studies on adaptive handover, risk-calibrated inference, and efficient TTS across different datasets, recording conditions, and hardware.
\end{description}

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:scope}

This study will focus on developing and evaluating a training-free, risk-calibrated method that chooses, for each utterance, what the teacher hands to the student in teacher-guided flow-matching text-to-speech (TTS), using DMOSpeech~2 and its F5-TTS teacher \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}. It will cover the reproduction of the released system, the two handover payloads, the candidate teacher paths, the routing rule, statistical calibration with Learn-Then-Test, and objective evaluation of speech quality, prosodic variety, and computing cost. Because only one teacher--student system will be studied, the findings may not transfer to other teacher-guided TTS systems.

The method will be adaptive in what the teacher hands over for each utterance, not in how much computation an utterance receives; its cost depends only on the teacher path, which is the same for every input; the candidate paths are compared in the evaluation. Choosing the amount of computation per utterance is outside the scope. The released system, which hands over at a switching time of 0.25 \parencite[p.~31819]{li2025dmospeech2}, will serve as the reference for cost and risk. The effect of the shorter teacher path alone will be measured by the version of the router that always hands over the teacher's estimate; the method will not be compared with step-pruning methods that shorten the teacher's own schedule for every input.

All pretrained model weights, including those of the teacher, the student, the duration predictor, and the vocoder, will be kept unchanged. Duration prediction will follow the published procedure and will not be part of the proposed contribution. Apart from the teacher path and the router's threshold, the settings of the method will be fixed before calibration, so that calibration chooses only among candidate paths and thresholds that are listed, and tested in an order that is fixed, beforehand.

The evaluation will use English zero-shot prompts from the Seed-TTS test set, which draws on Common Voice \parencite{anastassiou2024seedtts,ardila2020commonvoice}, divided by speaker into development, calibration, and test groups, and a second English dataset, LibriSpeech-PC \parencite{meister2023librispeechpc}, used only for testing. Prompts will be used as recorded and with added damage, such as background noise, room echo, and telephone-band filtering. Other languages, other kinds of recording damage, and very different inputs are outside the scope. Speech quality will be measured only with automatic measures (WER, SIM, and UTMOS). Prosodic variety will be measured from the variation in pitch across repeated generations of the same sentence; this captures only part of what listeners hear as varied speech, since it ignores rhythm and loudness.

The reliability guarantee will hold only under the statistical assumptions of the calibration procedure. It will limit how often the method fails where the released system does not, on average across inputs drawn from the same population and the same mix of prompt conditions as the calibration data; it will not guarantee acceptable quality for every individual utterance or for every prompt condition separately, and results on the second dataset will be reported as a check of transfer rather than as part of the guarantee. In addition, running time will be measured only on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU through Google Colab. The study will not assess clinical effectiveness, user experience, communication outcomes, or actual use in mobile assistive technologies. Listening tests with people will also be outside the scope of the study, except for a small check by the research team.

\subsection{Definition of Terms}
\label{sec:terms}

The following terms are defined as they are used in this study.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=2pt,font=\normalfont\itshape]
\item[Calibration.] Setting the router's threshold for each teacher path on a separate group of speakers, not used for the final evaluation, so that the risk is bounded with a stated confidence.
\item[Candidate teacher path.] One of a few predefined shortened teacher paths, each taking fewer, larger steps than the released system and costing fewer forward passes; each is calibrated and evaluated separately.
\item[Corrected payload.] A version of the teacher's estimate that keeps the order in time of its values in each frequency band but takes the values themselves from the draft, so that the teacher's timing is kept and copied recording conditions are removed.
\item[Draft.] The student's quick, one-step attempt at the speech, made from noise; it supplies the values for the corrected payload and one of the router's signals.
\item[Excess failure.] An utterance on which the method fails the quality-failure rule while the released system does not.
\item[Flow matching.] A way of generating data in which a network learns the direction in which random noise should move, step by step, to become speech.
\item[Forward pass.] One evaluation of a neural network, also called a neural function evaluation. Computing cost is counted in forward passes, with a guided teacher step counting as two.
\item[Handover.] The point in generation at which the teacher stops and the student takes over; also called the switching time.
\item[Learn-Then-Test.] The statistical procedure used to set the router's threshold for each teacher path so that the risk stays below the tolerance with a stated confidence, under its assumptions.
\item[Payload.] What the teacher hands to the student at the handover: its own estimate of the final speech or the corrected payload. No weights, gradients or separate features are passed.
\item[Payload router.] The rule that chooses the payload for each utterance from the prompt SNR and the residual.
\item[Prompt.] The short recording of a speaker given to the system, whose voice the generated speech should match.
\item[Prompt SNR.] An estimate, in decibels, of how loud the speech in the prompt is compared with its background noise.
\item[Prosodic variety.] How much the pitch of the speech varies across repeated generations of the same sentence; the property that teacher guidance was introduced to restore.
\item[Quality failure.] An utterance whose speech is unacceptable under a rule on word error rate, speaker similarity and UTMOS that is fixed before calibration and applied equally to every system.
\item[Released system.] DMOSpeech~2 run as released, with a fixed handover and 21 forward passes per utterance; the reference for cost and risk.
\item[Reliability guarantee.] The statement produced by calibration that, with probability at least $1-\delta$, the risk of every calibrated path is at most $\alpha$; it holds on average over inputs, not for each utterance.
\item[Residual.] A measure of how far the teacher's estimate is from its corrected version; it is zero when the two differ only in the order of their values in time, and grows when the teacher's loudness values have moved away from the draft's.
\item[Risk.] The expected rate of excess failures over new inputs.
\item[Speaker similarity.] How closely the voice in the generated speech matches the reference recording, measured by a speaker-verification model.
\item[Student model.] The smaller, distilled model that finishes generation in a few steps; in this study, the DMOSpeech~2 generator.
\item[Teacher model.] The larger, multi-step model whose outputs the student was trained to imitate; in this study, F5-TTS.
\item[Teacher's estimate.] The teacher's prediction of the final mel-spectrogram, computed at any step from the current state and velocity without an extra network evaluation.
\item[Teacher-guided sampling.] Generation in which the teacher performs the first steps and the student completes the output.
\item[Training-free.] Describes a method that changes only how the released models are run and does not train or fine-tune any network.
\item[UTMOS.] A model that predicts the mean opinion score listeners would give an utterance; it is used as an estimate of naturalness.
\item[Velocity.] The direction and rate in which the teacher moves the mel-spectrogram at a given step. It is the network's output, not the gradient of a training loss.
\item[Word error rate.] The share of words that a speech recognizer gets wrong when it transcribes the generated speech, compared with the input text; it measures intelligibility.
\item[Zero-shot TTS.] Speech generation in the voice of a speaker heard only through a short prompt, without training the model on that speaker.
\end{description}
