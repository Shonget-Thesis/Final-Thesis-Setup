\section{INTRODUCTION}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Background of the Study}

Text-to-speech (TTS) technology converts written text into spoken output while aiming to preserve intelligibility, speaker identity, and naturalness. One approach used in generative TTS is flow matching, which models a continuous transformation from a noise distribution to a speech representation. Flow-matching models learn a time-dependent vector field along a path connecting noise and speech, often using relatively simple or approximately straight paths. The learned field is then numerically integrated to generate speech \parencite{lipman2023flow,liu2023flow}. F5-TTS, developed by \textcite{chen2024f5}, applies flow matching through a Diffusion Transformer and introduces Sway Sampling to modify the sampling trajectory during inference. These developments provide a foundation for investigating ways to generate speech more efficiently, particularly when the computational cost of full teacher-model inference may be unnecessary for every input.

Despite these advances, speech generation can still require repeated neural-network evaluations, increasing computational cost and potentially affecting response time, which matters in interactive applications such as communication aids. Knowledge distillation addresses this challenge by training a smaller or faster student model to approximate a more computationally expensive teacher. \textcite{li2024dmospeech} developed DMOSpeech, a zero-shot TTS system that applies distribution-matching distillation and reduces the sampling process from 128 steps to four. Similarly, \textcite{zhu2025zipvoice} introduced ZipVoice, a flow-matching TTS system with a distilled version designed to accelerate speech generation. These studies demonstrate how distillation can reduce inference requirements. However, the efficiency of a distilled model does not establish that a single inference configuration provides the most suitable balance between computational cost and speech quality for every input.

Building on this direction, \textcite{li2025dmospeech2} developed DMOSpeech~2, a teacher-guided TTS system that combines the initial generation steps of a teacher model with a distilled student model that completes synthesis. The authors report that this approach improves output diversity compared with student-only generation while retaining an efficiency advantage over full teacher inference. This establishes a direct precedent for combining teacher guidance and distilled generation during TTS inference. However, the handover from the teacher to the student is governed by a predefined switching time rather than a mechanism that selects the switching time separately for each utterance. Consequently, the amount of computation allocated to the teacher remains fixed even when different inputs may have different requirements. Investigating whether the handover time can be selected adaptively may therefore provide a way to adjust computational effort according to the input while retaining the released sampling procedure of the distilled student.

Adaptive teacher-student collaboration has also been investigated in other generative tasks. \textcite{starodubcev2024your} proposed a method for text-to-image diffusion in which a student first generates an image and a quality estimator determines whether additional teacher-based refinement is needed. This approach demonstrates how teacher computation can be applied selectively rather than uniformly across generated outputs. \textcite{mualem2026aria} introduced ARIA, which adapts the allocation of training effort according to teacher-student discrepancies across conditioning regions. Although these studies provide relevant examples of adaptive computation and resource allocation, they address different tasks or stages of model development. \textcite{starodubcev2024your} focus on image refinement during inference, whereas \textcite{mualem2026aria} concerns the distillation process itself. The reviewed sources therefore provide related concepts but do not establish a method for selecting the teacher-to-student handover time for individual utterances in flow-matching TTS.

An adaptive handover policy also raises the question of how its decisions can be evaluated for reliability. Selecting a switching time according to inference-time signals does not, by itself, establish how often the selected configuration may introduce additional quality failures. Learn-Then-Test (LTT), introduced by \textcite{angelopoulos2025ltt}, provides a statistical framework for selecting among a finite set of candidate configurations while ensuring, with a chosen confidence level, that the selected configuration's risk does not exceed a specified tolerance, provided the framework's assumptions are satisfied. Applying LTT to teacher-guided TTS requires defining a quality-failure event, choosing a reference configuration, specifying the acceptable risk tolerance, and using appropriate calibration data. Its application in this study is intended to provide statistical control over the measured rate of additional quality failures, rather than to guarantee the quality of every individual utterance.

The reviewed literature provides the foundations for this study through flow-matching TTS, distilled speech generation, teacher-guided sampling, adaptive computations, and statistical risk calibration. However, within the sources examined, no single method has been established that combines per-utterance selection of the teacher-student handover time with statistical calibration of the additional quality-failure risk in flow-matching TTS. This gap is limited to the combination of these elements in the literature reviewed and does not imply that no other adaptive inference methods exist. The study therefore investigates whether an adaptive handover policy can improve the allocation of teacher computation while maintaining a statistically controlled level of additional quality failures.

To address this problem, the study proposes a training-free, risk-calibrated adaptive handover method using the DMOSpeech~2 distilled model \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}. The proposed method uses signals available during inference to select among candidate handover times while keeping the pretrained model weights unchanged. Rather than optimizing the student's restart level as an active variable during inference, the model preserves its standard, pre-defined default configuration. LTT is used to calibrate eligible handover configurations against a predefined tolerance for additional quality failures relative to a reference configuration, subject to the framework's statistical assumptions. The method is evaluated using word error rate (WER), speaker similarity (SIM), predicted naturalness (UTMOS), neural function evaluations (NFEs) or forward-pass counts, and runtime. These measures are used to determine whether adaptive handover scheduling can reduce computational cost while controlling the measured risk of additional quality failures.

Efficient and responsive speech generation may also be relevant to assistive information and communication technology for autistic users. Research on augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), including speech-generating devices, has examined their role in supporting communication among autistic individuals \parencite{therrien2025sgd,muharib2024sgd}. A text-to-speech component can provide spoken output for text selected or entered by a user, making inference efficiency a relevant consideration for communication-support applications. In this study, the ASD assistive-technology context motivates the practical relevance of developing a more computationally efficient TTS method. The primary evaluation, however, focuses on synthesis quality, computational cost, and the statistical control of additional quality failures; it does not assess clinical outcomes or establish the effectiveness of the resulting application as an AAC intervention. Experiments are conducted on a Ryzen~7 laptop with 16~GB of RAM and integrated graphics, while deployment on mobile or other resource-constrained devices remains a possible direction for future work.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

DMOSpeech~2 combines the initial generation steps of a teacher model with a distilled student model that completes speech synthesis, handing over at a predefined switching time \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}. However, a fixed handover may not provide the most suitable balance between speech quality and computational cost for every utterance. Selecting the handover time adaptively raises further questions about its effects on the generated speech and about the reliability of the resulting decisions. These concerns are relevant to efficient text-to-speech (TTS) for potential communication-support applications, including those intended for autistic users. Specifically, this study seeks to address the following problems.

\subsubsection{Uncharacterized Switching-Time Effects}
The teacher-to-student handover changes the generation process: the teacher integrates a guided flow, whereas the student is a distilled few-step generator that completes generation from the teacher's state \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}. Changing the switching time therefore changes the state the student receives. DMOSpeech~2 reports a single teacher-guided configuration with results averaged over its test sets \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}, so how different switching times affect intelligibility, speaker similarity, predicted naturalness and computational cost, and how these effects vary across utterances, requires empirical investigation.

\subsubsection{The Handover Boundary in Teacher-Guided Sampling}
A predefined switching time determines how much computation is allocated to the teacher before the student completes synthesis \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}. Because each guided teacher step requires two network evaluations \parencite{ho2022cfg}, a switching time later than an utterance needs spends computation that may not be required, while an earlier one may degrade speech. It remains to be determined whether selecting among candidate switching times using inference-time signals can reduce computational cost while maintaining speech quality across different utterances.

\subsubsection{Absence of Reliability Guarantees for Adaptive Handover}
Adaptive selection alone does not establish the rate of additional quality failures relative to a fixed reference switching time. Learn-Then-Test provides a framework for statistically controlling risk when selecting among candidate parameters, subject to its assumptions \parencite{angelopoulos2025ltt}. Its applicability to adaptive TTS handover must therefore be examined using a defined quality-failure criterion, calibration data separate from the evaluation data, and a prespecified risk tolerance.

\subsubsection{Application Constraints in Speech Synthesis for Neurodivergent Users}
Research has examined speech-generating devices as communication-support tools for autistic individuals \parencite{therrien2025sgd,muharib2024sgd}. However, the potential relevance of TTS to these applications does not establish that a more computationally efficient inference method improves communication outcomes. The technical performance of the proposed method must therefore be evaluated without assuming its usability or effectiveness as an assistive intervention.

In view of these problems, this study investigates whether a training-free, risk-calibrated adaptive handover method can reduce computational cost in teacher-guided flow-matching TTS while keeping the additional quality-failure risk, relative to a fixed reference switching time, within a prespecified tolerance.

\subsection{Objectives of the Study}

The general objective of this study is to develop and evaluate a training-free, risk-calibrated method for selecting the teacher's switching time per utterance in teacher-guided flow-matching text-to-speech (TTS) inference, reducing computational cost while keeping the additional quality-failure risk within a prespecified tolerance under frozen-model constraints. Specifically, this study aims to:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Characterize the switching-time space.} Measure how word error rate (WER), speaker similarity (SIM), predicted naturalness (UTMOS), forward-pass counts and runtime vary across candidate switching times and input utterances using the released teacher-guided sampling procedure, while retaining the student's default restart level.
\item \textbf{Formulate and analyze the adaptive selection method.} Define per-utterance switching-time selection as a constrained cost-minimization problem, develop a training-free selection policy that uses inference-time signals without modifying pretrained model weights or adding network evaluations, and analyze its theoretical properties.
\item \textbf{Calibrate the method for reliability.} Develop and implement a Learn-Then-Test calibration procedure that controls the additional quality-failure risk relative to a fixed reference switching time within a prespecified tolerance and confidence level, and assess the resulting guarantee on held-out evaluation data.
\item \textbf{Benchmark the quality--cost trade-off and robustness.} Compare the calibrated method with fixed switching times, an SNR-only selection rule and an offline oracle using WER, SIM, UTMOS, forward-pass counts and runtime under clean and noisy input conditions, and examine how the computational savings and the reliability guarantee vary with calibration sample size.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Significance of the Study}
The results of this study are expected to benefit the following:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=2pt,font=\normalfont\itshape]
\item[Speech synthesis researchers.] The study treats the teacher--student handover as a selectable and analyzable part of the inference algorithm, and provides a problem formulation, provable properties and a calibration procedure that apply to teacher-guided samplers in general, not only to the baseline examined here.
\item[TTS system developers.] The method offers a way to lower inference cost with a stated bound on the additional quality failures it introduces, without retraining or modifying any network.
\item[Assistive-technology developers.] Lower and more predictable inference cost supports speech-generating applications that rely on personalized voices; the study provides the synthesis component only and does not evaluate users or interventions.
\item[Future researchers.] The study documents a reproducible protocol for evaluating per-utterance inference decisions, including speaker-disjoint calibration, a predefined failure criterion and exact cost accounting, which can be reused for other adaptive sampling methods.
\end{description}

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}

This study covers the per-utterance choice of the teacher’s switching time in teacher-guided sampling for flow-matching text-to-speech. Its scope includes the decision signals used to make that choice, the selection policy and its analysis, the calibration of the policy’s threshold and the resulting reliability guarantee, and the objective evaluation of speech quality and computational cost. The switching time is the only handover setting that the study varies.

All network weights are kept fixed, including those of the teacher, the distilled student, the duration predictor and the vocoder; no model is trained or fine-tuned. Duration prediction is not part of the contribution and is used as published. The student’s restart level is likewise kept at the released code's default for every switching time, so that differences between candidate switching times reflect only how long the teacher runs.

The evaluation uses English zero-shot prompts from the Seed-TTS test set, which draws on Common Voice \parencite{anastassiou2024seedtts,ardila2020commonvoice}, under clean and noisy prompt conditions. The findings may therefore not transfer to other languages, speaking styles or recording conditions. Speech quality is assessed with objective measures, namely word error rate, speaker similarity and predicted naturalness.

The reliability guarantee applies statistically to inputs drawn from the same distribution as the calibration data and does not ensure reliability for every individual utterance. The guarantee may not remain valid when the characteristics of the input speech differ significantly from those represented in the calibration data. Computational efficiency is evaluated using the number of neural-network forward evaluations (NFE) and wall-clock runtime, with experiments conducted on a Ryzen 7 laptop equipped with 16 GB of RAM and integrated graphics; no measurements are made on mobile or other resource-constrained devices.

Finally, although the study is motivated by communication-support applications for autistic users, it does not build or evaluate any such application. Learner, clinical and educational outcomes, as well as the usability of the method in assistive settings, are outside the scope of this study.


\subsection{Definition of Terms}
The following terms are defined as they are used in this study.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=2pt,font=\normalfont\itshape]
\item[Text-to-speech (TTS).] The generation of spoken audio from written text. In this study, a zero-shot TTS system also receives a short recording of a speaker, the prompt, and produces speech in that speaker's voice without speaker-specific training.
\item[Flow matching.] A generative modeling approach that learns a time-dependent velocity field which moves random noise at time $t=0$ toward data at time $t=1$; generation integrates this field step by step \parencite{lipman2023flow}.
\item[Teacher model.] The large, multi-step flow-matching model whose outputs the student was trained to imitate. In this study, the teacher is F5-TTS \parencite{chen2024f5}.
\item[Student model.] The smaller distilled model that completes generation in a few steps. In this study, the student is the DMOSpeech~2 generator \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}.
\item[Teacher-guided sampling.] Generation in which the teacher performs the first denoising steps and the student completes the output \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}.
\item[Classifier-free guidance.] A technique that combines a conditional and an unconditional prediction of the model to follow the input text and prompt more closely \parencite{ho2022cfg}; each guided teacher step therefore requires two network evaluations.
\item[Switching time.] The point on the flow time axis at which the teacher stops and the student takes over, denoted $t_h$; it is also called the handover point. It is the only handover setting the study varies.
\item[Candidate switching time.] One of a small, predefined set of switching times among which the method chooses for each utterance.
\item[Reference switching time.] The fixed switching time used by the baseline configuration, against which the cost and quality of every other choice are compared.
\item[Restart level.] The point on the student's own time schedule at which it begins generation. It is kept at the released code's default in this study.
\item[Endpoint estimate.] A prediction of the final mel-spectrogram from an intermediate step, $\hat x_1(t) = x_t + (1-t)\,v(x_t,t)$, computed from the current state $x_t$ and velocity $v$ without an additional network evaluation.
\item[Endpoint-stabilization score.] The relative change of the endpoint estimate between two consecutive teacher steps, computed over the generated frames only; a small score means the teacher's prediction of the final output has stopped changing.
\item[Prompt signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).] An estimate, in decibels, of how loud the speech in the prompt is relative to its background noise, computed from frame energies of the prompt audio.
\item[Adaptive handover policy.] The rule that selects a switching time for each utterance from the endpoint-stabilization score and the prompt SNR, falling back to the reference switching time when no earlier candidate qualifies.
\item[Forward pass.] One evaluation of a neural network, also called a neural function evaluation (NFE). Computational cost is counted in forward passes, with a guided teacher step counting as two.
\item[Runtime.] The wall-clock time needed to synthesize an utterance on the study's hardware, also reported as the real-time factor, the ratio of synthesis time to audio duration.
\item[Word error rate (WER).] The proportion of words that an automatic speech recognizer transcribes incorrectly in the generated speech, compared with the input text; it measures intelligibility.
\item[Speaker similarity (SIM).] The cosine similarity between speaker embeddings of the generated speech and of the reference recording; it measures how closely the voice is reproduced.
\item[UTMOS.] A model that predicts the mean opinion score listeners would give an utterance \parencite{saeki2022utmos}; it is used as a proxy for naturalness.
\item[Added failure.] An utterance for which the output at a candidate switching time is clearly worse than the output at the reference switching time on the same input, according to a word-error and speaker-similarity criterion fixed before calibration.
\item[Risk.] The expected rate of added failures over the population of inputs.
\item[Calibration.] Choosing the policy's threshold on a held-out set of utterances, separate from the evaluation data, so that the risk is bounded with a stated confidence.
\item[Learn-Then-Test (LTT).] A statistical framework that selects among a finite set of candidate parameters by testing whether each one's risk exceeds a tolerance, with error control under its assumptions \parencite{angelopoulos2025ltt}.
\item[Reliability guarantee.] The statement produced by calibration that, with probability at least $1-\delta$, the risk of the selected threshold is at most a tolerance $\alpha$; it holds on average over inputs, not for each utterance.
\item[Oracle.] A diagnostic, non-deployable rule that picks, in hindsight, the cheapest candidate switching time that does not fail for each utterance; it marks the largest saving any risk-free policy could achieve.
\end{description}
