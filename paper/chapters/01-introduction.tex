\subsection{Background of the Study}
\label{sec:background}

Giving computers a voice is the goal of text-to-speech (TTS) technology, which turns written text into spoken output while aiming to keep the speech clear, true to the speaker's voice, and natural. One approach used in generative TTS systems is flow matching, which describes a smooth change from random noise into a representation of speech. Flow-matching models learn how to move along a path that connects noise to speech, and this path is often fairly simple or close to a straight line. The learned directions are then followed through a series of calculations to produce speech \parencite{lipman2023flow,liu2023flow}. F5-TTS, developed by \textcite{chen2024f5}, uses flow matching through a Diffusion Transformer and introduces Sway Sampling, which places more of the solver's steps early in generation, where the overall structure of the speech is decided. These developments form a basis for studying ways to generate speech more efficiently, especially because a model's full computing cost may not be needed for every input.

Despite these advances, generating speech can still require many repeated calculations by the neural network. This increases computing cost and may slow the response, which matters in interactive applications such as communication aids. Knowledge distillation addresses this challenge by training a smaller or faster student model to imitate a more computationally expensive teacher. \textcite{li2024dmospeech} developed DMOSpeech, a zero-shot TTS system that uses distribution-matching distillation and reduces the generation process from 128 steps to four. Similarly, \textcite{zhu2025zipvoice} introduced ZipVoice, a flow-matching TTS system with a distilled version designed to speed up speech generation. Within flow-matching TTS, \textcite{zheng2025epss} identified redundant sampling steps in F5-TTS and removed them without retraining, although the same pruned schedule is applied to every input. The cost is also higher than the step count alone suggests, because classifier-free guidance requires two forward passes at each step \parencite{ho2022cfg,yang2025shallow}. These studies show how distillation and step reduction can lower the computing needs of speech generation. However, neither the efficiency of a distilled model nor a single pruned schedule shows that one setting gives the best balance between computing cost and speech quality for every input.

Building on this direction, \textcite{li2025dmospeech2} developed DMOSpeech~2, a teacher-guided TTS system in which a teacher model carries out the first generation steps and a distilled student model completes the speech. At the handover, the teacher does not pass its weights, its gradients or a reduced set of features to the student. Both models work on the same mel-spectrogram with the same text and speaker prompt, and what the teacher hands over is its current estimate of the final mel-spectrogram, which it computes from its velocity at no extra cost \parencite{li2025dmospeech2code,lipman2024guide}. In this approach, the teacher runs fewer steps than in its full generation process, approximately 6 to 14 instead of 32 \parencite[p.~31818]{li2025dmospeech2}. The authors report that this approach improves output diversity compared with using the student alone, while remaining more efficient than running the full teacher \parencite[p.~31818]{li2025dmospeech2}. This provides a direct precedent for combining teacher guidance and distilled generation in TTS. However, the handover from the teacher to the student follows a predefined switching time rather than a method that selects the switching time separately for each utterance. Although the authors suggest lower switching times, around 0.1 to 0.2, for applications where efficiency matters and higher ones where diversity matters \parencite[p.~31818]{li2025dmospeech2}, the chosen value is applied to every utterance, and results are reported as averages over the test set. As a result, the amount of computing assigned to the teacher stays fixed, even though different inputs may have different needs. Studying whether the handover time can be selected adaptively may therefore offer a way to adjust computing effort to each input while keeping the published generation procedure of the distilled student.

Adjusting computation separately for each input has been studied in other generative tasks. In flow-matching image and video generation, \textcite{bajpai2026fastflow} and \textcite{bajpai2026coflow} choose sampling steps for each input without retraining, noting that \enquote{a fixed step budget may be unnecessarily conservative for some inputs while insufficient for others} \parencite[p.~3]{bajpai2026coflow}. \textcite{zhang2025adadiff} likewise select a step count for each prompt in diffusion models, although their selection policy must be trained. Closer to a teacher--student setting, \textcite{starodubcev2024your} proposed a method for generating images from text in which a student first creates an image and a quality estimator decides whether the teacher needs to refine it further. This approach shows how teacher computing can be used selectively rather than equally for all outputs. \textcite{mualem2026aria} applied a related idea during training rather than generation, giving more training samples to groups of prompts where the student differs most from the teacher. In speech, \textcite{hsieh2025adflowtse} allocate more flow-matching steps to noisier inputs in target speaker extraction, although their estimate of the noise level comes from a trained predictor. These studies show that computation can be assigned according to the input, but none of them addresses speech synthesis or a handover from a teacher to a distilled student during generation. The reviewed sources therefore provide related ideas but do not establish a method for selecting the teacher-to-student handover time for individual utterances in flow-matching TTS.

An adaptive handover policy also raises the question of how reliable its decisions are. Choosing a switching time based on signals available at inference does not, by itself, show how often the chosen setting may introduce additional quality failures. Statistical risk control has already been applied to adaptive computation: \textcite{schuster2022calm} calibrated early exiting in language models, \textcite{jazbec2024fast} applied risk control to early exiting in diffusion-based image generation, and \textcite{damri2026confident} used Learn-Then-Test to choose, for each input, how many recognition hypotheses to pass to a language-model corrector in speech recognition. \textcite{wu2025thought} likewise used Learn-Then-Test to decide when a language model can stop reasoning. These studies show that statistically calibrated speed-ups are feasible, but they address text, images, or speech recognition rather than speech synthesis. Learn-Then-Test (LTT), introduced by \textcite{angelopoulos2025ltt}, is a statistical framework for choosing among a limited set of candidate settings while making sure, at a chosen confidence level, that the risk of the chosen setting does not go beyond a stated limit, as long as the framework's assumptions are met. Applying LTT to teacher-guided TTS requires defining what counts as a quality failure, choosing a reference setting, stating the acceptable risk limit, and using suitable calibration data. In this study, LTT will be used to control the measured rate of additional quality failures, not to guarantee the quality of every individual utterance.

The reviewed literature provides the foundations for this study through flow-matching TTS, distilled speech generation, teacher-guided sampling, adaptive computation, and statistical risk calibration. However, within the sources examined, no method has been established that selects the teacher-to-student handover time for each utterance and statistically calibrates the added risk of quality failures in flow-matching TTS. Related adaptive and risk-controlled methods exist for image generation, language models, and speech recognition, so this gap is limited to this specific combination in the literature reviewed and does not mean that no other adaptive methods exist. The study will therefore investigate whether an adaptive handover policy can improve how teacher computing is assigned while keeping the added quality failures at a statistically controlled level.

To address this problem, the study will develop a training-free, risk-calibrated adaptive handover method that uses the distilled model of DMOSpeech~2 by \textcite{li2025dmospeech2}. The method starts from the handover itself. DMOSpeech~2 treats the switching time as a single setting chosen once for all inputs; this study treats it instead as a decision made separately for each utterance, and states that decision as a problem of using as few forward passes as possible while keeping the rate of added quality failures below a limit set beforehand. The proposed method will use two signals available at inference, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the speaker prompt and the change in the teacher's estimate of the final output between consecutive steps, to choose among candidate handover times while keeping the pretrained model weights unchanged. Because flow matching makes that estimate available at every teacher step from the velocity that has already been computed, reading the second signal costs no forward pass. Instead of adjusting the student's restart level as an active variable during generation, the method will keep it at its standard, predefined default setting. LTT will be used to calibrate the threshold of this stopping rule so that the rate of additional quality failures, relative to a reference setting, stays within a predefined limit, subject to the framework's statistical assumptions, using a definition of an added quality failure written for TTS. Chapter~3 shows that the method never costs more than the released sampler and that its decision adds no forward pass. The method will be evaluated using word error rate (WER), speaker similarity (SIM), predicted naturalness (UTMOS), neural function evaluations (NFEs) or forward-pass counts, and runtime. These measures will be used to determine whether adaptive handover scheduling can reduce computing cost while controlling the measured risk of additional quality failures.

Efficient and responsive speech generation may also be relevant to assistive information and communication technology for autistic users. Research on augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), including speech-generating devices, has looked at their role in supporting communication among autistic individuals \parencite{muharib2024sgd,therrien2025sgd}. A TTS component can provide spoken output for text that a user selects or types, which makes the efficiency of speech generation relevant to communication-support applications. In this study, the assistive-technology context of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) motivates the practical value of developing a more computationally efficient TTS method. The main evaluation, however, will focus on speech quality, computing cost, and the statistical control of additional quality failures. It will not assess clinical outcomes or establish how effective the resulting application is as an AAC intervention. Throughout the study, the goal is a lightweight inference method: less computation for each utterance, with speech quality kept within a limit stated beforehand. Experiments will use an NVIDIA Tesla T4 graphics processing unit (GPU) through Google Colab, while use on mobile or other devices with limited computing power remains a possible direction for future work.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Speaking without delay matters especially when a person relies on a device to communicate, because users of augmentative and alternative communication already produce messages far more slowly than their speaking partners \parencite{yusufali2023rate}. DMOSpeech~2, developed by \textcite{li2025dmospeech2}, produces speech by letting a teacher model carry out the first generation steps and a distilled student model finish the speech. The switch between the two happens at a predefined time, so every utterance receives the same amount of teacher computing. However, a fixed handover may not give the best balance between speech quality and computing cost for every utterance. Lowering this cost per utterance is useful only if the quality it may lose is controlled. These concerns matter for efficient text-to-speech (TTS), including its possible use in communication-support applications, such as those intended for autistic users.

To begin with, the handover changes the way speech is generated. In the released implementation of DMOSpeech~2, the student takes the teacher's estimate of the final result at the handover and combines it with fresh noise according to its restart setting \parencite{li2025dmospeech2code}. Changing the switching time therefore changes what the student receives. DMOSpeech~2 reports a single teacher-guided setting, with results averaged over its test sets \parencite[p.~31819]{li2025dmospeech2}, so how different switching times affect speech quality and computing cost, and how these effects differ from one utterance to another, needs to be tested through experiments, particularly in terms of intelligibility, speaker similarity, and predicted naturalness.

Building on this, the handover point itself determines the computing cost. A predefined switching time decides how much computing the teacher does before the student finishes the speech \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}. The authors themselves note that a lower switching time of around 0.1 to 0.2 can be used with minimal degradation in perceptual quality \parencite[p.~31818]{li2025dmospeech2}, which suggests that the default switching time may be later than some utterances need. Because each guided teacher step requires two forward passes \parencite{ho2022cfg,yang2025shallow}, a switching time that is later than an utterance needs may spend computing that is not required, while one that is too early may harm the speech. In the released sampler, the fixed switching time of 0.25 stops the teacher at step~8 of its 16-step time grid, so every utterance costs 21 forward passes, 18 of them spent by the teacher \parencite{li2025dmospeech2code}. This fixed point acts as a cap on teacher computation: the teacher always runs up to it, whether an utterance needs it or not. Handing over at teacher step 4, 5, 6 or 7 instead would cost 13, 15, 17 or 19 passes, which is 38\%, 29\%, 19\% or 10\% less. The researchers' preliminary experiment on 500 speakers (Appendix~\ref{app:prelim}) found that the cheapest switching time that kept quality differed between utterances, and that choosing it for each utterance in hindsight, among steps 4, 5, 6 and 8, would need 14.85 passes on average, 29\% fewer than the fixed setting; on noisy prompts, however, every fixed early switching time added quality failures to about half or more of the inputs. It is still unclear whether choosing among candidate handover times for each utterance, using signals available at inference, can lower computing cost without increasing quality failures. Such a saving is also usable only if the quality it may lose is known before use. A selection rule tuned on data does not, by itself, show how often it will add quality failures compared with the fixed reference setting. Statistical risk control has been applied to adaptive computation in other tasks, such as language models, image generation, and speech recognition \parencite{damri2026confident,jazbec2024fast,schuster2022calm,wu2025thought}, but within the sources reviewed, not to the teacher-to-student handover in TTS. Learn-Then-Test provides a framework for setting a decision threshold among candidate settings so that the risk stays within a stated limit, as long as its assumptions are met \parencite{angelopoulos2025ltt}. An adaptive handover method therefore needs its selection rule to be calibrated in this way, using a defined quality-failure criterion, calibration data, and a risk limit set beforehand.

Finally, these technical concerns connect to the use of speech synthesis for autistic users. Research has examined speech-generating devices as communication-support tools for autistic individuals \parencite{muharib2024sgd,therrien2025sgd}. However, the possible relevance of TTS to these uses does not show that a more computationally efficient method improves communication outcomes. The technical performance of the proposed method must therefore be evaluated without assuming that it is usable or effective as an assistive intervention.

In view of these concerns, the general problem addressed by this study is whether a training-free, risk-calibrated adaptive handover method can reduce computing cost in teacher-guided flow-matching TTS while keeping the added risk of quality failures, compared with a fixed reference setting, within a limit set beforehand. Specifically, the study seeks to answer the following research questions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item How do different switching times at or before the published reference affect speech quality (intelligibility, speaker similarity, and predicted naturalness) and computing cost, when the student restarts from the teacher's estimate combined with fresh noise at its default restart setting, and how do these effects differ across utterances?
\item Can a training-free rule that chooses among candidate handover times for each utterance, using signals available at inference and with its threshold calibrated by Learn-Then-Test, lower computing cost while keeping the risk of additional quality failures, relative to a fixed reference setting, within a limit set beforehand, without additional network evaluations or changes to the model weights?
\item How does the calibrated adaptive method compare with fixed switching times, a rule based only on prompt SNR, and an offline oracle in speech quality, computing cost, and the rate of additional quality failures under clean and noisy prompt conditions; does the observed failure rate on held-out speakers stay within the limit; does the endpoint-stabilization signal add value beyond the prompt SNR; and how do its savings and risk control vary with calibration sample size?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Objectives of the Study}
\label{sec:objectives}

\subsubsection{General Objective}
The general objective of this study is to develop and evaluate a training-free, risk-calibrated method for choosing the teacher-to-student switching time for each utterance in teacher-guided flow-matching text-to-speech (TTS), aiming to lower computing cost while keeping the rate of additional quality failures, compared with a fixed reference setting, within a risk limit set beforehand, with all model weights kept unchanged. In other words, the method looks for the earliest, and therefore cheapest, switching time that keeps the rate of added quality failures within that limit, not the latest or highest-quality one.

\subsubsection{Specific Objectives}
Specifically, this study aims to:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Describe the handover settings.} Measure how word error rate (WER), speaker similarity (SIM), predicted naturalness (UTMOS), forward-pass counts, and runtime change across switching times at or before the published reference and across input utterances, using the released DMOSpeech~2 teacher-guided sampling procedure while keeping the student's default restart level.
\item \textbf{Develop the calibrated adaptive selection method.} Define the choice of switching time for each utterance as a problem of minimizing cost while keeping the risk of additional quality failures within a set limit; develop a training-free selection rule that uses signals available at inference, without changing the pretrained model weights and without additional network evaluations for computing those signals; calibrate the rule's threshold with Learn-Then-Test (LTT) so that this risk, compared with a fixed reference setting, stays within a limit and confidence level set beforehand; and analyze the properties of the resulting method.
\item \textbf{Evaluate the method and test its robustness.} Compare the calibrated adaptive method with fixed switching-time configurations, a rule based only on the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the prompt, and an offline oracle that chooses the cheapest acceptable switching time for each utterance in hindsight, using WER, SIM, UTMOS, the rate of additional quality failures, forward-pass counts, and runtime under clean and noisy prompt conditions. Check whether the observed rate of additional quality failures on held-out speakers stays within the set limit, determine whether the endpoint-stabilization signal adds value beyond the prompt SNR, and examine how computational savings and the reliability guarantee vary with calibration sample size.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Significance of the Study}
\label{sec:significance}

This study investigates a training-free, risk-calibrated adaptive handover method for teacher-guided flow-matching text-to-speech (TTS). Its findings may contribute to more efficient speech synthesis while keeping the rate of additional quality failures within a risk limit set beforehand. The results are expected to benefit the following:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=2pt,font=\normalfont\itshape]
\item[TTS researchers.] This study may provide insights into how the choice of switching time affects speech quality and computing cost, and how much this varies from one utterance to another, supporting further research on adaptive TTS inference.
\item[TTS developers.] The proposed method may offer a way to choose the switching time for each utterance without retraining the pretrained models and without additional network evaluations, potentially reducing computing cost while controlling the rate of additional quality failures. Because the method falls back to the reference switching time whenever no earlier handover is certified, it is designed never to cost more than the original sampler.
\item[Researchers in reliable machine learning.] The application of Learn-Then-Test (LTT) may demonstrate how statistical risk control can be used to calibrate the stopping threshold of an adaptive inference rule in a generative model, under stated calibration assumptions.
\item[Assistive technology and AAC developers.] The findings may inform future research on efficient TTS components for communication-support technologies, including augmentative and alternative communication (AAC).
\item[Autistic and neurodivergent learners.] The study may indirectly support future assistive-technology research by examining the technical performance of speech synthesis. However, it does not directly evaluate usability or communication outcomes for these learners.
\item[Future researchers.] The methodology and findings may serve as a reference for further studies on adaptive handover, risk-calibrated inference, and efficient TTS across different datasets, noise conditions, and hardware, including a calibrated handover threshold, reported with the conditions under which it holds, that can serve as a starting point for teacher-guided TTS.
\end{description}

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:scope}

This study will focus on developing and evaluating a training-free, risk-calibrated method for choosing the teacher-to-student switching time for each utterance in teacher-guided flow-matching text-to-speech (TTS), using DMOSpeech~2 and its F5-TTS teacher \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}. It will cover the choice of candidate switching times, two decision signals available during generation (the prompt signal-to-noise ratio and the change in the teacher's estimate of the final output between steps), the selection rule, statistical calibration with Learn-Then-Test, and objective evaluation of speech quality and computing cost. Because only one teacher--student system will be studied, the findings may not transfer to other teacher-guided TTS systems.

The switching time will be the only handover setting that the study varies. The switching time of 0.25 used by DMOSpeech~2 \parencite[p.~31819]{li2025dmospeech2} will serve as the reference, and only switching times at or before it will be studied. Switching times later than the reference will not be tested, because each later handover adds guided teacher steps and therefore computing cost, so it cannot make the method more efficient than the published setting it builds on. Which of the earlier switching times can serve as handover points, including the earliest ones, will be determined through the experiments of the first objective rather than fixed in advance.

All pretrained model weights, including those of the teacher, the student, the duration predictor, and the vocoder, will be kept unchanged. Duration prediction will follow the published procedure and will not be part of the proposed contribution. The student's restart level will be kept at its released default for every switching time, so that differences between candidates reflect only how long the teacher runs.

The evaluation will use English zero-shot prompts from the Seed-TTS test set, which draws on Common Voice \parencite{anastassiou2024seedtts,ardila2020commonvoice}, under clean and noisy prompt conditions, with calibration data kept separate from evaluation data. \todo{R13: if an unseen dataset is added, name it here and in Section 3.4.} Therefore, the findings may not apply to other languages, other recording conditions, or very different inputs. Speech quality will be measured only with automatic measures: word error rate (WER), speaker similarity (SIM), and predicted naturalness (UTMOS). The study will not measure prosodic diversity, the property that teacher-guided sampling was designed to improve.

The reliability guarantee will hold only under the statistical assumptions of the calibration procedure. It will limit the added risk of quality failures on average across inputs drawn from the same distribution as the calibration data, and it will not guarantee acceptable quality for every individual utterance. The guarantee may not hold when the inputs differ from the calibration data. In addition, running time will be measured only on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU through Google Colab, and no measurements will be made on mobile or other limited-resource devices. Furthermore, the study will evaluate only technical performance and computing efficiency. It will not assess clinical effectiveness, user experience, communication outcomes, or actual use in mobile assistive technologies. Listening tests with people will also be outside the scope of the study, except for a possible small diagnostic check by the research team.

\subsection{Theoretical Framework}
\label{sec:theoretical}

The study tests three claims, one for each research question:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item A fixed teacher--student handover spends teacher computation that many utterances do not need, and the cheapest switching time that keeps quality differs from one utterance to another (Research Question~1, Objective~1).
\item A per-utterance stopping rule built from signals the sampler already computes can lower the number of forward passes without adding network evaluations or changing any model weights, and Learn-Then-Test can bound its rate of added quality failures at a confidence level stated before calibration (Research Question~2, Objective~2).
\item The calibrated rule saves computation compared with the fixed handover under clean and noisy prompts while its observed failure rate on held-out speakers stays within the limit, and the comparison with simpler rules shows whether the endpoint-stabilization score adds value beyond the prompt SNR (Research Question~3, Objective~3).
\end{enumerate}
Each claim may turn out false; the methodology is designed so that a negative result is detected and reported (Section~\ref{sec:workflow}).

This study rests on four bodies of theory, summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:theoretical}, each read through the principles of text-to-speech: how flow-matching TTS turns noise into a mel-spectrogram, how teacher-guided TTS divides that work between two models, how a TTS system can give each utterance only the computation it needs, and how the reliability of such a decision can be stated. Each one supplies one part of the proposed method, and together they explain why an earlier handover can be safe for some utterances and how that safety can be checked.

\paragraph{Flow-matching speech synthesis} In flow-matching TTS, the mel-spectrogram of an utterance is generated by moving random noise toward speech along a learned velocity field, conditioned on the text and the speaker prompt, from time $t=0$ (noise) to $t=1$ (speech) \parencite{lipman2023flow,liu2023flow,mehta2024matcha}. The models used in this study are trained on straight paths between noise and data. Because the paths are straight, one step of the remaining length along the current velocity gives an estimate of the final output from any point along the way, $\hat x_1(t)=x_t+(1-t)\,v(x_t,t)$, without any extra network evaluation \parencite{lipman2024guide}. F5-TTS, the teacher in this study, follows this approach \parencite{chen2024f5}. This theory gives the study two things: the endpoint estimate that the teacher hands to the student, and a way to observe, during generation, whether the teacher's prediction of the final speech has stopped changing. If it has, more teacher steps are expected to add little.

\paragraph{Teacher-guided TTS} In zero-shot TTS, knowledge distillation trains a fast student model to reproduce the speech of a slower teacher \parencite{yin2024dmd,li2024dmospeech}. A distilled student is cheap but tends to lose some variety in its outputs, which DMOSpeech~2 restores by letting the teacher run the first steps and the student finish \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}. The reasoning behind this division of labor is that the early, noisy steps decide the coarse structure of the speech, such as timing and prosody, while the later steps refine acoustic detail \parencite{chen2024f5,li2025dmospeech2}. At the handover, the student mixes the teacher's estimate with fresh noise before continuing; studies of this kind of re-noising show that it keeps the overall layout of the guiding input while correcting smaller errors \parencite{meng2022sdedit,xu2023restart}. Because each guided teacher step costs two network evaluations \parencite{ho2022cfg} and each student step costs one, the switching time largely decides the cost of an utterance. This theory explains why the handover point is where computation can be saved, and why the student can tolerate a teacher state that is not yet perfect.

\paragraph{Per-utterance computation} Utterances differ in how hard they are to synthesize: texts differ in length, speakers in rate, and prompt recordings in quality, and noisy prompts are known to degrade flow-matching zero-shot TTS \parencite{wang2024noise}. Input-adaptive computation holds that a system can save computation by giving each input only as much processing as it needs and stopping once a signal shows that enough has been done \parencite{graves2016act,teerapittayanon2016branchynet}. Confident Adaptive Language Modeling applied this idea to language generation and used, among others, a signal that measures whether the model's internal state has stopped changing \parencite{schuster2022calm}. In this study, the same idea becomes a stopping rule for the teacher. At each candidate step, the rule checks the prompt SNR and the endpoint-stabilization score, and stops the teacher only when they indicate that an early handover is safe; otherwise the teacher continues to the reference step, so the rule never costs more than the fixed setting.

\paragraph{Reliability of an inference setting} A TTS system that stops the teacher early will sometimes produce worse speech, so it needs a way to state how often this happens. Distribution-free risk control chooses the setting of a rule from held-out calibration data so that its expected rate of errors, the risk, stays below a chosen tolerance $\alpha$ with probability at least $1-\delta$ \parencite{bates2021rcps}. Learn-Then-Test does this by treating each candidate threshold as a hypothesis, ``the risk of this threshold is above $\alpha$'', and accepting only the thresholds for which the calibration data give strong evidence against that hypothesis \parencite{angelopoulos2025ltt}; testing the thresholds in a fixed order keeps the overall chance of error at $\delta$ \parencite{bauer1991}. The result is a guarantee of the form $\P(R\le\alpha)\ge1-\delta$, which holds on average over new inputs drawn like the calibration inputs, not for every single utterance. This theory supplies the calibration step and the reliability guarantee.

Taken together, these theories form the reasoning of the study. Flow matching makes the state of generation observable at every teacher step at no extra cost; teacher-guided sampling makes the handover point the main lever on cost; per-utterance computation suggests setting that lever separately for each utterance; and statistical risk control makes it possible to state, before use, how often doing so will add a quality failure. The study tests whether this reasoning holds in practice for teacher-guided flow-matching TTS.

\subsection{Conceptual Framework}
\label{sec:conceptual}

The conceptual framework, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:conceptual}, applies the theories of Section~\ref{sec:theoretical} to the teacher-guided sampler of DMOSpeech~2 \parencite{li2025dmospeech2}. It is drawn as the architecture of the proposed method, so that its components, the process each utterance goes through, and the outputs of the study can be read from one figure.

The components fall into three groups. The frozen backbone, made up of the duration predictor, the F5-TTS teacher, the DMOSpeech~2 student and the Vocos vocoder, generates speech exactly as released, and its weights are never changed. The stopping controller is added between the teacher and the student, and the offline calibrator runs once before use. For each utterance, the input is a recorded speech prompt with its transcript and the text to be spoken. The duration predictor sets the length of the output, and the teacher takes guided steps from noise toward speech. At each candidate step, the controller reads two values the sampler already has: how much the teacher's estimate of the final mel-spectrogram has changed since the previous step, and the signal-to-noise ratio of the prompt. If both pass the controller's threshold, the teacher stops and hands its estimate to the student; otherwise it takes another step, at most up to the reference step of the released sampler. The student completes the mel-spectrogram in three steps, and the vocoder turns it into a waveform. Before any of this, the calibrator sets the controller's threshold $\hat\lambda$ on a held-out calibration set with Learn-Then-Test, using a tolerated risk $\alpha$ and a confidence parameter $\delta$ fixed in advance.

The outputs are the speech for each utterance, the switching time chosen for it together with its cost in forward passes, and the calibrated threshold with a stated bound on the rate of added quality failures. The study produces these outputs on clean and moderately noisy prompts from the Seed-TTS test set and compares them with the released sampler and the other methods of Objective~3. Section~\ref{sec:architecture} describes each component in full, and Section~\ref{sec:workflow} gives the steps by which the objectives produce these outputs.

\subsection{Definition of Terms}
\label{sec:terms}

The following terms are defined as they are used in this study.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=2pt,font=\normalfont\itshape]
\item[Adaptive handover policy.] The rule that chooses a switching time for each utterance from the prompt SNR, the endpoint-stabilization score, or both, and uses the reference switching time when no earlier candidate qualifies.
\item[Added quality failure.] An utterance whose speech at a candidate switching time is clearly worse than at the reference switching time on the same input, judged by a word error rate and speaker similarity rule fixed before calibration.
\item[Calibration.] Choosing the policy's threshold on a separate set of utterances, not used for the final evaluation, so that the risk is bounded with a stated confidence.
\item[Candidate switching time.] One of the small, predefined set of switching times among which the policy chooses for each utterance.
\item[Cap.] The fixed point at which the baseline always hands over from the teacher to the student ($s=0.25$, teacher step 8, 21 forward passes); the same point as the reference switching time.
\item[Endpoint estimate.] The teacher's prediction of the final mel-spectrogram, computed at any step from the current state and velocity without an extra network evaluation. It is the only thing the teacher hands to the student: no weights, gradients or separate features are passed.
\item[Endpoint-stabilization score.] How much the endpoint estimate changes between two consecutive teacher steps; a small score means the teacher's prediction of the final speech has settled.
\item[Flow matching.] A way of generating data in which a network learns the direction in which random noise should move, step by step, to become speech.
\item[Forward pass.] One evaluation of a neural network, also called a neural function evaluation. Computing cost is counted in forward passes, with a guided teacher step counting as two.
\item[Learn-Then-Test.] The statistical procedure used to choose the policy's threshold so that the risk stays below the tolerance with a stated confidence, under its assumptions.
\item[Oracle.] A reference rule, not usable in practice, that picks in hindsight the cheapest candidate switching time that does not fail for each utterance; it shows the largest possible saving.
\item[Prompt.] The short recording of a speaker given to the system, whose voice the generated speech should match.
\item[Prompt SNR.] An estimate, in decibels, of how loud the speech in the prompt is compared with its background noise.
\item[Reference switching time.] The fixed switching time of the baseline setting, against which the cost and quality of every other choice are compared.
\item[Reliability guarantee.] The statement produced by calibration that, with probability at least $1-\delta$, the risk of the chosen threshold is at most $\alpha$; it holds on average over inputs, not for each utterance.
\item[Restart level.] The point on the student's own schedule at which it starts generating from the teacher's estimate mixed with fresh noise; it is kept at the released default in this study.
\item[Risk.] The expected rate of added quality failures over new inputs.
\item[Speaker similarity.] How closely the voice in the generated speech matches the reference recording, measured by a speaker-verification model.
\item[Student model.] The smaller, distilled model that finishes generation in a few steps; in this study, the DMOSpeech~2 generator.
\item[Switching time.] The point in generation at which the teacher stops and the student takes over; also called the handover point.
\item[Teacher model.] The larger, multi-step model whose outputs the student was trained to imitate; in this study, F5-TTS.
\item[Teacher-guided sampling.] Generation in which the teacher performs the first steps and the student completes the output.
\item[Training-free.] Describes a method that changes only how the released models are run and does not train or fine-tune any network.
\item[UTMOS.] A model that predicts the mean opinion score listeners would give an utterance; it is used as an estimate of naturalness.
\item[Velocity.] The direction and rate in which the teacher moves the mel-spectrogram at a given step. It is the network's output, not the gradient of a training loss, and the endpoint estimate is computed from it.
\item[Word error rate.] The share of words that a speech recognizer gets wrong when it transcribes the generated speech, compared with the input text; it measures intelligibility.
\item[Zero-shot TTS.] Speech generation in the voice of a speaker heard only through a short prompt, without training the model on that speaker.
\end{description}
